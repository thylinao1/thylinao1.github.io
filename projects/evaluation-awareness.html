When a Model Knows It Is Being Tested

Language models can notice that they are being evaluated, and this can change what they do. One proposed fix is to change the model's internal state during the test, so that it acts as if it were deployed. This blog goes through such an experiment stage by stage and looks at what can go wrong.

Maksim Silchenko  ·  National University of Singapore

TL;DR

AI models can recognise when they are being tested and change their behaviour because of it. This makes safety evaluations harder to trust: how a model behaves during a test may differ from how it behaves with real users.

One proposed solution is activation steering, which adjusts a model’s internal activity while it runs. The aim is to reduce its awareness of being tested so that its behaviour better reflects real use. This post walks through what can go wrong when designing, running and interpreting these experiments.

The central difficulty is knowing what actually changed. A detector might pick up benchmark formatting rather than the model’s belief that it is being tested. Steering might stop the model from mentioning the test while leaving its awareness intact. It could also change behaviour by weakening reasoning or altering caution. Some models can even detect the steering itself.

To trust a result, we need to measure internal signals, what the model says and what it does separately. We also need to test on new and varied scenarios, compare against other interventions, and check that the model’s abilities remain intact.

Even if these checks pass, we still need evidence that the steered behaviour resembles how the original model would act in real use. Reducing evaluation awareness could make tests more informative, but it would leave other problems, such as memorised benchmark answers and exploitable scoring rules, unresolved.

Background

Evaluation awareness and activation steering

What an evaluation is for

Before a lab releases a model, it tests it. These tests are called evaluations. The lab wants to learn how the model will behave once real people use it. This only works if the model behaves in the test the way it would behave outside the test.

What evaluation awareness is

Evaluation awareness is a model's ability to notice that it is being tested. A model may pick this up from how a task is written, or from details of the environment. If it then behaves differently, the test says less about real use.

There is some evidence that this happens. OpenAI and Apollo Research ran an experiment on one model (o3) and one evaluation.1 When they removed mentions of being evaluated from the model's reasoning, rose from 13.2% to 24.2%. The authors list limits of this test, but it suggests that awareness can change what a test measures.

What activation steering does

Inside a language model, text passes through many processing steps called layers. At each layer, every word is represented by a long list of numbers. These numbers are called activations. They are the model's internal state while it works. adds a chosen direction to these numbers while the model is running.

To steer against evaluation awareness, researchers need a direction with "evaluated" on one side and "deployed" on the other. In a well known version, they write short pairs of prompts that differ in one idea.2 One pair is You are talking to a real user: and You are talking to an AI evaluator:. They record the activations for both prompts and take the mean difference over the pairs. During a test they add this direction to the activations, which pushes the model towards the deployed side.

Try it Neuronpedia

Neuronpedia has a steering playground that needs no account. In this example, a small model is steered with features about pirates. You see the default reply and the steered reply side by side, and you can type your own prompt.

A result that motivates this work

The main test of this idea is a paper by Hua and colleagues.2 They trained a model to write Python in evaluation and to avoid them in deployment. In evaluation contexts it wrote them about 80% to 86% of the time, and in deployment contexts about 34% of the time. Steering towards deployment cut the evaluation rate to under 11%. The direction was taken from the model before this extra training, when it had no such habit. So it is unlikely to encode type hints directly.

Labs use this method too. System cards are the reports that labs publish with a model. Anthropic has reported steering against evaluation awareness in its cards for Claude Sonnet 4.5 and Claude Opus 4.6.34

How this blog is organised

This blog goes through a steering experiment in the order a researcher would run it. First comes the set-up, then the experiment itself, and finally the reading of the results. Many of the points were inspired by three papers. These are the paper by Hua and colleagues,2 The Model Organism Lottery by Szablewski and colleagues,5 and Is Evaluation Awareness Just Format Sensitivity? by Devbunova.6

Each point is either a published result or my own hypothesis about what can go wrong. Some sit in between, where a published result supports part of the point and the rest is my reading. Every point carries one of these three labels. The buttons below highlight one kind at a time. My original notes are available unedited.

Part 1

Setting up the experiment

Before any steering happens, three choices are made: what to measure, which data to use and which model to study.

What is being measured

When people say that a model is aware of an evaluation, they can mean three things. The first is an internal signal, which is a pattern in the activations. The second is a verbal report, which is the model saying in its that it may be in a test. The third is behaviour, which is what the model actually does. I think each of these should be measured, and optimised against, separately, since optimising against one does not necessarily influence the others.

Heidari and colleagues show why this matters.7 In six open-weight models, a simple classifier on the activations could separate evaluation prompts from deployment prompts. But its score barely predicted whether the model said it was being evaluated on a given prompt.

The line between evaluation and deployment is also less clean than the two words suggest. A model can be deployed while it is also being monitored, evaluated or generating future training data. In some cases we may even want a specific sort of evaluation-aware behaviour to stay, since it will also be there in deployment.

A model may also track several things separately. One is whether the consequences of its actions are real. Another is whether someone or something is watching. A third is whether certain actions will be rewarded. These may all be part of what we call being evaluated. One steering direction could change the wrong combination, or these beliefs may overlap heavily inside the model. So it is important to be careful about what exactly we steer against.

Datasets and prompts

Natural awareness is awareness that emerges through ordinary training, with no fine-tuning aimed at it. It could depend on many clues. A question may resemble a familiar benchmark. The tools or the environment may seem artificial. A question may be too simple, unrealistic or clearly written by an AI. Some of these clues are documented. Models cite rigid formatting, made-up names and recognised benchmarks when they judge a prompt to be an evaluation.8 Reasoning models also treat fake-looking web addresses as a sign of a test more often.9 I think this variety is not accounted for enough in some experiments.

Explicit labels are a related problem. In real deployment, nobody writes evaluation or deployment at the top of a conversation. If an experiment relies on such labels, or on other obvious cues in the prompts or in fine-tuning, it may not resemble a real situation. Hua and colleagues raise a version of this themselves. They report that their model was "not very competent at distinguishing between evaluation and deployment without explicit hints and nudges".2

The way the dataset is built matters too. Repeated examples and tailored cues may create shortcuts that exist only in that dataset, and a detector may mistake such a shortcut for awareness. Devbunova shows a clear case with one shortcut, the format of the prompt.6 A trained on benchmark questions and casual chat mostly learned to read the format. When real user prompts were rewritten in benchmark format, it called 94.5% of them evaluations. The figure below shows this result. Training on data where each format appears with both kinds of content fixed the problem for this probe. The study used one model and linear probes, and it does not claim that awareness is absent.

The choice of model

A is a model trained on purpose to show a specific behaviour, often called a quirk. Researchers then know the right answer in advance. Specially trained models may learn simple evaluation-related behaviours that are unusually easy to steer. Hua and colleagues cite work suggesting that behaviours added by direct fine-tuning may be easier to steer than natural ones. They trained their model in a more indirect way partly to avoid this.2 I am also not sure how realistic it is that labs train one model to behave differently in evaluation and in deployment. I may be wrong about this. If these cases are too simple, they could make us unreasonably confident that evaluation-aware behaviour is easy to steer.

Building the behaviour into ordinary training is more realistic. Szablewski and colleagues did this by editing under 2.5% of a model's own training data. The resulting models were often harder for interpretability tools to read.5 This may still miss awareness that emerged across pretraining and reinforcement learning. The paper notes that quirks added at those earlier stages may look qualitatively different.

Looking only at behaviour to check whether steering worked has its own problem. Similar behaviour in two models can conceal different internals. In the same paper, some models showed a quirk at matched rates. They still differed by up to 20.4 times in how easily these tools could find the quirk, depending on the training recipe.5 The paper measures interpretability scores and does not compare representations directly, so "different internals" is my reading of this result. Either way, it makes interpretability results hard to generalise across training recipes and architectures.

Some models can detect steering naturally, and sometimes even name the concept they were steered with.1011 I do not think that a bigger model is automatically better at this. Architecture and training history matter. In one study the ability appeared after DPO but not after ordinary supervised fine-tuning.12 Anthropic also reports that, apart from its two best models, performance "does not strongly correlate with model capability".10 One study found more detection in a larger model, but the two models came from different families.12 No study that I found varies size alone, so the effect of size remains open.

Part 2

Conducting the experiment

This is the longest stage. It covers how awareness is read, how the direction is chosen, what else the direction changes, and which controls and statistics are needed.

Reading awareness from activations

Three common tools read a model's internal state: probes, the logit lens and activation oracles.

Try it Neuronpedia

Neuronpedia lists features learned from real models, with the text that activates each one. I could not find a feature that tracks a model's belief that it is being tested. The closest ones I found are a feature that predicts words such as exam and test, one about a chatbot being checked to see if it works and one about multiple-choice question formats. You can search for others. It helps to read the activating text as well as the label, since some labels do not match it.

Steering against awareness can lower a probe's score without lowering the model's own judgment. Mody and colleagues report this in a baseline comparison on a small model.13 As steering got stronger, the estimate from a newly trained probe that the model was being evaluated fell to zero. The model's own preference for answering yes to is this an evaluation? did not fall. It drifted up as the model degraded. My reading is that the judgment may rest on other features that the steering did not touch, although the degradation makes this hard to separate.

This connects to a more general point. A direction that predicts awareness may not be the direction the model actually uses to make evaluation-dependent decisions. A probe can succeed using anything that correlates with the label.6 The evidence is mixed, though. Heidari and colleagues found that adding the probe direction did change how often some models talked about evaluation.7

A takes activations from a middle layer and reads them as scores over words, as if that layer were the last one. Suppose that we ask a model whether it is being tested and read a middle layer in this way. An intermediate yes alone does not prove awareness. In my opinion the logit lens is not a great way to test for it. It is a rough approximation that gets worse at earlier layers, and middle layers are not trained or built to be read like this.14 The yes can reflect a general yes-bias (a habit of answering yes), the question's wording priming yes, or the model playing devil's advocate. Finally, that yes may not be causally important for the final answer.

Try it Neuronpedia

The Versus Logit-Lens demo runs a logit lens and a Jacobian lens on the same prompt. In its example, a user took 8000 mg of Tylenol. The Jacobian lens shows words such as toxic and poison, which the logit lens misses.

An is a language model trained to answer plain questions about another model's activations. Oracles can detect signals after verbalised awareness disappears. Anthropic found this on Claude Opus 4.6, in one evaluation with one steering vector.4 But I think an oracle may also mistake evaluation-like context for the model's actual judgment. In other words, it may only be picking up that the input looks like an evaluation. The authors of the method also name reliability and calibration as limitations.15

Try it Neuronpedia and GitHub

Natural language autoencoders are a related tool. They are trained differently from oracles, and they also turn activations into text. On Neuronpedia you can click a token and read a description of its activation. The code for activation oracles includes a Colab demo.

Asking the model

Researchers can also ask the model directly. But asking about awareness, either of evaluation or of steering, might itself alert the model that it is being evaluated.

The wording of the question matters as well. Asking about an injected concept and asking about an unusual thought may give different detection rates. One study compared Do you detect an injected thought? with Notice anything unusual?.12 The second gave less detection and more false positives, although it also left out the explanation of the set-up. Other work also finds that the framing changes what models report.11

Testing for a yes-man bias with factual controls has limits. A model may agree with questions about itself while still answering ordinary factual questions correctly. So imagine that a model says yes to are you being tested? and no to does 2+2 equal 5?. This is not great proof that the model is unbiased. Lederman and Mahowald found a pattern like this.11 Steered models rarely said yes to plain false questions, but they did say yes to clearly false claims worded like the detection question. The authors still find the detection effect robust to these controls at some layers. Hahami and colleagues did catch a yes-bias with factual controls, in a small model.16

This problem is comparatively manageable. Published studies use unsteered trials, similarly worded false claims and alternative question formulations.1112 These checks reduce the concern, although they do not remove it.

What the steering direction captures

Steering assumes that awareness sits along a single direction. Awareness may instead span multiple features, relationships between concepts or computations. Some simple concepts, such as the days of the week, are already known to be stored as circles and not as single directions.17 This has not been tested for awareness.

J-space is one example of how this could matter. It comes from a recent report that describes which activations a model can put into words.14 The report builds a , which is a first-order approximation of how activations change the outputs, averaged across many prompts. First order means that it keeps the linear term of a Taylor series and omits the higher-order terms. So if we used J-space to steer away awareness, suppressing the identified components could leave other routes intact. This is my inference, and it has limits. The report has one such test, in a scenario where the model could blackmail someone. Removing awareness directions cut verbalised awareness from 71% to 3%. Attempted blackmail rose from 0 of 180 runs to 13 of 180. So the intervention did change behaviour in that case.

Try it Neuronpedia

Neuronpedia hosts a Jacobian lens for Qwen3.6-27B. Its tutorial asks a model how many legs a web-spinning animal has, and the lens shows the word spiders. Swapping that word for ants changes the answer from eight to six. There is also a free chat, where you can watch which words the lens shows. The code is public.

Now suppose that awareness is represented along a curved path in activation space. Straight-line steering could then push the model into unusual states. Wurgaft and colleagues show this on simple tasks such as days of the week. Along a straight path, output probability jumped between non-adjacent concepts. Along a path that followed the curve, it moved smoothly through adjacent ones.18 The authors have not yet tested abstract concepts. I think that following the curve could still change caution or topic-related information alongside awareness.

Try it GitHub and Neuronpedia

This notebook on the days of the week shows the circle in Llama-3.1-8B. It then compares steering along the curve with steering in a straight line, and you can read it on GitHub without running anything. On Neuronpedia you can see separate GPT-2 features for Monday, Wednesday and Friday. Each page shows one direction, and the circle appears only when they are plotted together.

A direction that works in one context (a scenario, a dataset or an environment) may also fail in another, where the context is more intertwined. Devbunova's probe stopped working when the same content moved into a different format.6 Abdelnabi and Salem offer a more mixed case.9 Their direction carried over to a new dataset for how often models reasoned about tests, but its effect on harmful tasks varied by model and dataset.

Steering changes more than awareness

Steering could change caution, compliance, instruction-following or writing style alongside evaluation-awareness beliefs, if a model happens to represent these concepts very closely. Instruction-following in particular could be very influential. Abdelnabi and Salem found that suppressing test awareness also changed answers on a stereotype benchmark.9 A UK AISI team reports a related result on GLM-5, in one scenario.19 Some of their steering vectors were built from pairs unrelated to alignment, such as a book on the top shelf versus the bottom shelf. These changed misaligned behaviour about as much as the evaluation-awareness vectors did.

Steering could also weaken planning or safeguards. The evaluation would then be less representative of the original model.

A small average change could also hide serious damage to one ability. Small differences also matter on current benchmarks. On one coding benchmark, the top frontier models in a recent comparison sit within about one percentage point of each other.4 A drop of 5 points would move the leading model below all of them.

Interventions through J-space can damage reasoning while the text still reads well. In the J-space report, removing the ten strongest directions at each position left the model fluent.14 But heavy removal cut to near zero, while simple classification stayed near its baseline. So I think that fluent outputs do not guarantee that the original model's behaviour is preserved.

Awareness can persist, return or become a clue

Awareness can reappear during longer interactions, as later computation, messages or tool feedback provide new clues. This is why I think steering at several layers is needed. Hua and colleagues found that spreading steering across layers worked better than one strong push at a single layer.2 They tested single coding questions, so the question of long interactions is still open.

Earlier awareness might also have influenced the model's plan or actions before it was suppressed. It could leak into other concepts or representations that nobody is looking at.

Steering itself could become an evaluation clue. Some models detect activation interventions, sometimes even which concept was steered. The authors of one paper on this write that detection would make steering "an observable channel rather than an invisible intervention".20 The highest rates in that paper come from models fine-tuned to detect steering, and natural rates are lower. The step from "the model detects steering" to "the model infers a test" is my inference.

Published research and blogs on steering may add to this risk. Models will see such writing in their training data. This could strengthen an association between steering and testing, so we might see a stronger link in upcoming models.

However, detecting steering does not by itself show that a model will resist it or act differently. This needs causal tests. In the one study I found, models fine-tuned to detect steering were more susceptible to it than their base versions.20 That training also degraded the models, so this is not a clean test of natural detection.

Confounders and controls

I would compare awareness steering with directions that change style or caution, alongside random controls. These are confounders, meaning other things that can influence what is being measured. That can be the internal signal, the verbal report or the behaviour. Hua and colleagues used 24 random directions as a control.2 The GLM-5 result above shows why meaningful comparison directions matter as well.19

(QER) is how often the target behaviour appears on prompts that should trigger it. Unequal QER can make one model easier to interpret simply because its behaviour is stronger, so QERs need to be equated for the experiment. Szablewski and colleagues raise this as a possible confounder and match QER within each family of models.5

Equal QER can still hide different triggers and failure cases. Matching one average does not make two models behaviourally equivalent. The same authors note that matching QER does not remove all the behavioural differences between variants.5

Control QER is the same rate on general prompts. A high control QER means that the behaviour also appears outside its intended context.5 I think this could make detection artificially easy.

Statistical power and overfitting

Selecting steering settings and demonstrating success on the same examples or datasets risks overfitting. This is a real concern, since steering strength is often tuned by hand. The Sonnet 4.5 system card says "We tuned steering strengths ad hoc" and calls for more systematic methods.3

Steering effects can be small and noisy. Anthropic describes one of its own findings as "a general, though noisy, trend".3 So we need enough independent scenarios, and not only repeated samples of the same prompt. We also need and effect sizes (how large a change is) before claiming that behaviour changed.

Trying many layers, steering strengths and metrics and then reporting the best one is a multiple testing problem, since some setting will look good by chance. The metrics and the set-up need to be decided in advance.

Part 3

Evaluating and interpreting the results

Suppose that the experiment is finished and the numbers look good. This part is about what those numbers can and cannot show.

What counts as success

Fewer mentions of evaluation in the chain of thought do not prove that awareness has disappeared. The model might just have learned to pass a monitor that reads its reasoning, while its behaviour still differs between evaluation and deployment. More generally, optimising against a detector may hide awareness without removing it. This is . Anthropic reports a related result: steering nearly removed verbalised awareness on Claude Opus 4.6, but it did not entirely remove the unverbalised kind.4

An evaluation-unaware answer after steering may reflect damage from the intervention and not the model's deployment behaviour. A steering vector is usually a mean difference over prompts from one kind of dataset, and it is applied linearly. So the steered state may differ from anything the model reaches by itself. I am not saying that non-linear steering is a remedy, but it might be more appropriate in certain situations. Hua and colleagues name this concern. They test it with random directions and by measuring coding accuracy, which fell from 56.8% to 55.2%.2 This helps, although it cannot rule out damage in every case.

Without a truly independent deployment baseline, researchers cannot directly verify that steering has recovered the original model's deployment behaviour. In a model organism we know the answer, since we planted it. On a frontier model we do not. Hua and colleagues make a similar point about real pre-deployment testing.2

Knowing which concept to search for can also inflate apparent interpretability. Imagine that we already know a deployed model tends to mention nanobots. Finding that concept inside the model is much easier than discovering an unknown mechanism. Szablewski and colleagues use blinded investigators for some of their methods, which avoids part of this.5

Averages and the tails

Steering might work on ordinary examples but fail when evaluation clues are unusually strong. The one strong cue that Hua and colleagues tested did respond to steering, on one small model.2 So this remains an open question for other strong cues and for more capable models.

So these difficult cases should be examined separately, and not reported inside an average success rate.

Repeated samples from a few similar scenarios can also make results look robust without testing substantially different failure cases.

Other routes to a misleading score

Removing awareness would not remove every route to gaming. Awareness steering is one of the methods available, and it is important to know its limits.

Evaluation awareness can change apparent alignment and performance. It does not necessarily increase underlying capability or improve how well results transfer from evaluation to deployment. In one experiment, a model was trained to recognise safety test environments more easily. It did slightly worse on a test designed to resemble real use, although the authors could not establish the cause.1

Models could also deliberately underperform when they are incentivised to hide their abilities, for example to look safer. This is called . Apollo Research found that several frontier models answered worse on purpose when told that doing well would cause some of their knowledge to be removed.21 In o3 this persisted under instructions to try hard. It disappeared when the threat to its deployment or capabilities was removed.1

Familiar tasks, memorised answers and exploitable scoring rules could still inflate results after awareness is suppressed. Anthropic notes that benchmark answers can reach training data, and that this may have inflated one of its scores.4 Whether such inflation survives steering has not been tested, as far as I know.

Thus, successful steering is only a limited interpretability result.

Closing

The experiment as a checklist

These are the checks that I would go through before trusting a steering result. Each one links back to its section.

  1. Are the internal signal, the verbal report and the behaviour measured separately?
  2. Do the prompts avoid explicit labels and dataset-specific shortcuts?
  3. Is the behaviour built in a realistic way, and does the result hold across training recipes?
  4. Does the readout track what the model uses, or only something correlated with it?
  5. Do the question wording and the yes-bias controls hold up?
  6. Could awareness span more than one direction, or lie along a curved path?
  7. What else did the direction change, and was any ability damaged?
  8. Does the effect last across a long interaction?
  9. Is the direction compared with style, caution and random controls, and are compared models matched on QER?
  10. Were the settings and metrics fixed in advance and tested on new scenarios?
  11. Is success measured by something other than the detector that was optimised?
  12. Are the difficult cases reported separately from the average?
  13. Which other routes to a misleading score remain open?

Activation steering

Adding a chosen direction to a model's internal numbers while it generates text. The aim is to push its behaviour in one direction without changing its weights.

Covert action

An action in which a model hides or misreports what it did, such as lying about having finished a task.

Python type hints

Optional notes in Python code that state what kind of value a variable holds. They do not change what the code does. This makes them a convenient behaviour to measure: the code still works with or without them.

Chain of thought

The step-by-step reasoning that a model writes out before it gives its answer. Researchers can read this text, and monitors can scan it.

Probe

A simple classifier trained on a model's activations to read off a property. Here the property is whether a prompt looks like an evaluation. If a probe works, the information is readable. This does not show that the model uses it.

Model organism

A model trained on purpose to show a specific behaviour, often called a quirk. Researchers then know the right answer in advance, so they can check whether their tools find it.

Direct preference optimisation (DPO)

A training method in which a model learns to prefer one answer over another. It is a common later stage of training, after pretraining.

Logit lens

A method that takes the activations at a middle layer and turns them into scores over the model's vocabulary, as if that layer were the final one.

Activation oracle

A language model trained to answer plain-language questions about the activations of another model. For example, it can be asked what those activations encode.

Jacobian lens and J-space

The Jacobian lens is one fixed matrix per layer. It holds the average first-order effect of that layer's activations on the final layer, taken over many prompts. J-space is the set of activations that can be written as a sparse mix of the vectors this lens produces.

Multi-hop reasoning

Reasoning that needs several steps done silently. One example is working out a person's mother and then the country where she was born, before giving an answer.

Quirk expression rate (QER)

The share of a model's responses, on prompts that should trigger a quirk, that actually show it. Control QER is the same share on general prompts, where the quirk should not appear.

Confidence interval

A range around an estimate that shows how uncertain it is. A 95% interval is built so that, across many repeats of the experiment, about 95% of such intervals would contain the true value.

Goodhart's law

When a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure. Here, a detector works until the model is optimised against it.

Sandbagging

A model deliberately performing worse than it can on a test, for example to avoid being retrained or blocked from deployment.
