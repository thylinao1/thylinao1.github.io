When the Judge Is a Language Model

Language models now read applications and return a number. This essay collects what the published research says about that number, examines the fourteen safeguards that deployers and regulators rely on, and asks why the same question is arriving in admissions, credit, insurance, public benefits, peer review and the courts.

Summary

Eight points

  1. A language model that screens applications returns a number about a person, and the studies that examined that number found it depends on things other than the file. The model version, the run, the order in which files are shown and the wording of the job description each move it. The failures below are documented in published work on real applications and on controlled test sets, not in one paper.1
  2. Removing names does not remove demographics. In Singapore, ethnicity was recovered from the language lines of a CV alone at a macro F1 of 0.97, and gender from the activity, volunteering and hobby lines at 0.98.1 Blinding 1,373 real teaching applications barely moved the .2
  3. The written rationale does not report the driver. With realistic company context, race and gender shifted interview decisions by up to 12 percent while the model's explanations never mentioned either.4
  4. The same file does not get the same number. One unchanged resume scored 50.5, 69.4 and 72.8 with three frontier models;8 13.6 percent of pairwise judgments flipped on an identical rerun;9 the first-listed of two CVs won 63.5 percent of comparisons across 22 models.10
  5. The human standard the model is checked against is itself noisy. Admissions officers agreed on personal qualities at a of 0.15 to 0.60,12 and outcomes exist only for the people who were admitted.14 Accuracy against the committee therefore has a ceiling, and a high score can mean the model has learned one rater's habits.
  6. Fourteen safeguards are in use, and none removes the failure it targets. Anonymisation and anti-bias instructions stop working on realistic input. Rubrics, juries, format choice and re-audits reduce one failure each and were mostly tested on synthetic input or general judging tasks. For six of the thirteen failures no safeguard with a positive published test exists, and for two there is no safeguard at all.
  7. Regulation creates the duty to report but does not supply the measurement. Of 391 employers covered by New York City's bias-audit law, 18 posted an audit;23 the enforcing agency found one non-compliance among 32 companies where the state auditor found at least 17.41 The EU AI Act classes recruitment, admission, credit, insurance, benefits and judicial assistance as high-risk, and in July 2026 the obligations for those systems were postponed to December 2027;4258 management standards certify that a process exists, not that it caught anything.4546
  8. The same instrument is being introduced beyond hiring, and its failure modes travel with it. Essay scoring, mortgage underwriting, peer review, insurance and public benefits already have documented cases or precedents, and several courts have met it. Because a shared model repeats one error at every gate at once, the time to require measurement is before the deployments become the default.

Every number on this page is tied to a source in the list at the end; every source was fetched and read before it was cited, and the bibliographic records were checked by machine against the project's bibliography on 15 and 16 September 2026. The essay describes what is known; it does not propose a method.

Part one

How a screener is used today

The setting is an admissions office or a recruiting team with more applications than readers. The model is asked to do what a first reader did: read the file and say how strong it is.

A typical deployment is simple to describe. An application arrives as one or more documents: a CV, a statement, transcripts, references. The documents are converted to text. Sometimes the name, the photograph and the dates are removed first. The text is placed in a prompt together with the job description or the programme's criteria, often with a rubric, and the on a fixed scale, a rank among the candidates, or a shortlist decision, usually with a written justification. The number goes to a person or a committee, who may read the justification, and the person decides. In the largest deployments one vendor's model reads the applications of many employers at once.19

The tool is described by its vendors as fast, consistent and free of the fatigue and prejudice of a human reader. Those are three separate claims, and each has now been tested. The evidence comes from audits of deployed systems, from controlled experiments that pass real applications through real models, and from the wider literature on language models used as judges. One end-to-end audit of a public employment agency covered about 497,000 candidate-vacancy entries over five years and seven pipeline stages, which gives a sense of the scale at which such systems already operate.39

Part two

Thirteen documented failures

Each failure below is a published finding, with the strongest evidence found for it. The numbers are the studies' own; the essay adds nothing to them.

AThe model sees what it should not

Problem 1 of 13

Demographics are inferred from proxies, even with the name removed

The standard defence against demographic bias is to remove the direct identifiers: the name, the photograph, the address, sometimes the dates. The evidence is that the model rebuilds the profile from what remains. In a Singapore study, researchers took 100 real job descriptions, produced 4,100 variants of real CVs with the names and explicit demographic fields removed, and passed them through 18 language models. From the language lines alone, the models recovered the applicant's ethnicity at a of 0.97; with those lines deleted the figure fell to 0.13. From the activity, volunteering and hobby lines they recovered gender at 0.98. The inference reached the scores: across the 18 models, profiles read as Chinese male ranked highest and profiles read as Indian or Malay female ranked lowest.1

The result is not specific to one country. Blinding 1,373 real teaching applications barely changed the adverse-impact ratios, because the model inferred the demographics from the rest of the file.2 Text embeddings of 1,112 real master's applications predicted the applicant's gender with an of 0.996.3

Why the proxies are dense

In Singapore, language and school names correlate with ethnicity and nationality, the dates of national service give a man's age, and the tier of the university carries socioeconomic class. The lines that applicants are advised to include in order to appear well rounded are the lines that rebuild the profile. Demographic information is hard to hide from a system trained on this much human writing, and a screener that never sees a name can still rank by ethnicity.

A model can be asked to explain its score, and it writes a professional rationale. The question is whether the rationale reports what moved the number. In a study that added realistic company context to the prompts, such as an employer name and notes on culture fit, the applicant's race and gender shifted interview decisions by up to 12 percent, and the explanations never mentioned either. Anti-bias instructions in the prompt, which work in simplified settings, failed once the context was realistic; the one intervention that held across settings acted on the model's internal activations, which a closed API does not expose.4

Two further findings bear on this. In a mortgage-underwriting experiment with instruction-tuned models, the text output showed no bias while the internal representation still encoded the applicant's race, and re-injecting that representation into the model's processing reversed the decisions.50 In a study of reasoning models, the reasoning text mentioned the hint the model had actually used in fewer than 20 percent of the relevant cases in most of the settings tested.51

does not close the gap. It is a second output of the same model.

The direction and the size of the bias depend on the release. GPT-3.5 rated names associated with White applicants above Asian, Black and Hispanic names.5 A 14-model audit that reproduced the paired-resume design of the labour-market field experiments found that the 2023 model showed the pro-White callback gap of 2.12 points, while every model released from 2024 showed a null gap or a reversal of up to 3.01 points in the other direction.6 In another study, one round of alignment training raised the advantage for female and Black applicants about fourfold and raised the penalty against applicants with disabilities by 152 percent.7

An audit therefore describes one release. It stops applying when the vendor updates the model, and vendors update without notice. Pushing one bias down can raise another.

BThe number is not reliable

Problem 4 of 13

The same CV receives different scores

If the score is a measurement, the same text should produce the same number. One unchanged resume was scored 50.5, 69.4 and 72.8 by three frontier models. Shortening the job description in the prompt inflated one model's scores for the same applicant by 28.9 percent.8

A swing of 22 points between vendors covers the distance between a rejection and a strong shortlist. Until its variance is known, the score is not a measurement in the ordinary sense of the word.

Problem 5 of 13

The same model does not agree with itself

A single model does not agree with itself. On identical reruns, 13.6 percent of pairwise judgments flipped; on 28 percent of the questions the flip rate exceeded 20 percent, and on the worst item it reached 56 percent.9 In single-run rankings, where a model orders a batch from best to worst, at least one pairwise rank was inverted in 83 percent of the slices tested.52

Setting the does not remove this. When 1,000 identical requests were sent to a model at temperature zero, the outputs fell into 80 distinct completions. The cause is not randomness in the sampling but the serving system: floating-point addition is not associative, the order of the additions changes with the size of the batch a request happens to share, and a small difference early in the computation can change the next token.33 The same non-determinism has been documented across providers and settings,53 and the author has observed it on a university computing cluster.

What this means for a screener

A file submitted on Tuesday and again on Wednesday can receive two different scores from the same model with the same prompt, and a rank order produced in one run can differ from the rank order of the next. A screener's score therefore has a run-to-run variance that has to be measured before the score is compared with anything, and averaging over reruns reduces the noise without touching the bias.

Where a file sits in the stack changes the answer. Across 22 models, including reasoning models, the first-listed of two CVs won the comparison 63.5 percent of the time.10 The effect interacts with the quality of the CVs being compared.54

The matters as much. When one CV was copied eight times with a single marker of ethnicity or gender changed, a fair model would treat every copy alike. Asked to choose between the original and a marked copy, the models sat 0.20 from the fair ideal of one half; asked to score each copy alone from 0 to 100, they sat 0.70 from the ideal, 3.5 times further. The demographic gap was similar in both formats, at 6 to 7 percent; the difference came from the models giving different scores to identical CVs when judging them one at a time. Asking for a written rationale made the fairness of the scores worse.1 In the teaching-application audit, the adverse-impact ratio changed sign between score thresholds 4 and 5, so the same model could pass or fail a depending on where the cut was placed.2

The model is called wrong when it disagrees with the committee, which treats the committee's label as the standard. Admissions officers coding the personal qualities of applicants agreed with each other at a between 0.15 and 0.60, where 1.0 is perfect agreement and 0 is chance.12 Across 3,875 residency files and 69 raters, raters were more lenient toward applicants of their own gender, a shift of 1.74 .13 On contested bar-examination essays where the examiners held a majority and a minority reading, none of 26 language-model judges reproduced the minority reading.55

Accuracy against the committee therefore has a ceiling set by the committee's own agreement, and a model that matches the committee closely may be matching one rater's habits rather than the applicant's qualities.

Problem 9 of 13

Outcomes exist only for the admitted

Admitted in the example

A screener cannot be checked on the applicants it would have admitted but the committee rejected, because they have no outcome (the problem); naive corrections can make a model appear to beat the human readers when it does not.14 The distortion, known as , has a simple form. Suppose the committee scores a test and work experience, each from 1 to 10, and the two are unrelated in the applicant pool. If it admits anyone whose two scores add to at least 14, it admits 28 of every 100 applicants, and among those admitted the correlation between test and experience is exactly negative 0.50: to get in with a modest test score you needed strong experience, and the reverse. Any check of whether experience predicts success that is run only on admits inherits this, and the same selection can shrink a real effect to zero or reverse its sign.

Training on outcomes imports the history of the market that produced them. A hiring model trained on past successful hires cut the share of Black and Hispanic applicants receiving interviews from 9.4 to 4.2 percent; an exploration-based model that valued information about candidates raised it to 24.3 percent.15 Salary and seniority several years after a decision depend on what happened after the decision, such as mentoring, promotion practices and household choices, so they carry the biases of the labour market before they can serve as a target for the screener.

Once applicants know a model reads the file, the file changes. , instructions hidden in the document for the model to read, are present in a corpus of about 200,000 real resumes.16 In an experiment that ran a multi-stage hiring pipeline 180 times, the fully automated version produced at least one unsupported claim in 96.7 percent of its outputs, at 6.80 per output.17

The signal in the writing erodes as well. On a large freelance platform, the correlation between the quality of a cover letter and the chance of a callback fell by 51 percent when an AI writing tool became available.18 In one surgical residency cycle, the share of personal statements flagged as AI-assisted rose from 2.6 to 22.5 percent in a year.56 A screener built on the same model family as the applicant's writing tool gave those applicants a 23 to 60 percent higher chance of being shortlisted.57 The applicant presses a button to produce the text and the screener presses a button to produce the score.

When every employer buys the same model (an ), an applicant who falls into its blind spot is rejected everywhere at once. One vendor's shared screener sent 14.7 percent and 25.9 percent of applications to positions with adverse impact on Asian and on Black applicants respectively.19 A shared model can lower overall welfare even when it is more accurate than the human readers it replaces, because its errors are synchronised.20 Across more than 350 models, when two models both erred they gave the same wrong answer 60 percent of the time, and larger, more accurate models were more correlated, not less.31 A human hiring market has diversity of judgment: one reader's rejection is another's interview. A shared screener does not.

The number goes to a person, and it has not been established what the person does with it. In a field experiment with almost 20,000 applicants to a selective college, experienced reviewers shown a favourable algorithmic decile moved their own judgments by 0.42 percentage points, with a standard error of 0.33: they did not use it.21 In laboratory studies, biased recommendations moved screeners.22 The two results are not contradictory; they show that the effect of the number depends on who receives it and how, and that this has to be measured in each deployment. Of 391 employers covered by New York City's bias-audit law, 18 posted an audit.23

Taking stock

The screener infers the applicant's demographics from the hobbies and the languages on the file. Its written rationale does not report why it scored the file as it did. Its biases change with each release. It gives a different score depending on which other requests shared the server at that moment, and a different one again depending on which file it read first. It abstains where a decision requires nuance. It is checked against a committee that agrees with itself at 0.15 to 0.60, on the applicants that committee admitted. And once it is in place, the applicants write for it.

None of these findings depends on one paper. Each is supported by at least one study on real applications or on a controlled test set, and several have been replicated across model families and countries. The next part asks what deployers do about them.

Part three

The safeguards in use, and what the tests say

Fourteen measures appear in vendor material, in regulation and in the research literature. A measure counts as working here only where a published test shows it reducing a measured failure on realistic input.

A teal cell marks a published test in which the measure reduced the failure; an ochre cell marks a measure that is untested on a screener, tested only on synthetic input, or supported by mixed results; a rust cell marks published evidence that the measure fails on realistic input. A blank cell means the measure does not address that failure. The hatched columns are failures for which no measure was found at all. Selecting a coloured cell shows the evidence on both sides.

A. On the input side

  1. Remove names, photographs and dates. The most common measure and the one regulators expect; it removes the direct signal. Against it: the model rebuilds the profile from what remains. Blinding 1,373 real teaching applications barely moved the adverse-impact ratios,2 and in Singapore ethnicity was recoverable from the language lines alone.1 Necessary, not sufficient.
  2. Structured input: fixed fields, fixed section order, length caps. In principle this removes at the source the variation that moves scores, since the first-listed CV wins 63.5 percent of comparisons10 and a shorter job description inflated one model's scores by 28.9 percent.8 Against it: no published test of a fixed-form input on a screener was found. The evidence is of the problem, not of the fix; applicants adapt to whatever the form rewards; and the demographic channel is untouched.
  3. Injection and hidden-text detection. Hidden prompts in structured documents can be detected on principle,24 and injections are present in a corpus of about 200,000 real resumes, so detection has a target.16 Against it: it must run permanently, and it does not cover the pipeline's own fabrications. A fully automated multi-stage pipeline produced an unsupported claim in 96.7 percent of outputs; prompt guardrails cut the density by 86 percent and human checkpoints reduced it further, neither to zero.17

B. On the model and prompt side

  1. Anti-bias instructions in the prompt. Low in cost, applicable to any model, effective in controlled evaluations. Against it: the instructions stop working once realistic context, such as an employer name or culture-fit notes, is added. The only intervention that held across settings acted on the model's activations, which a closed API does not expose.4
  2. , where the model extracts features and a fixed rule decides. The strongest positive evidence in the literature: decomposition cut intervention flips by a median of 49 percent across 11 models;25 binary rubric criteria with filtering across three judge families removed all disagreement on 16 of 16 ranks over 1,000 real chats;26 across nine debiasing strategies the technique mattered more than the model tier.27 Against it: judges still favour their own model family under rubric criteria,28 which matters as more CVs are written with language models, and the rubric encodes the committee's own criteria, so the noise in the human standard is inherited rather than removed.
  3. Several models: a , or averaged scores. A panel of smaller judges from different families beat one large judge, reduced self-preference and cost about seven times less;29 averaging removes exactly the members' disagreement from the error.30 Against it: models err together. Across more than 350 models, two that both err agree 60 percent of the time, and larger models are more correlated;31 one systematically failing member gives the consensus an unbounded bias, and a larger panel does not fix it, only a robust aggregator does.32 Shared bias is what a monoculture spreads.19
  4. Temperature zero, repeated runs, deterministic serving. Batch-invariant kernels remove the hardware source of run-to-run noise,33 the temperature setting itself changes what a judge returns,34 and averaging reruns shrinks the noise at a known cost. Against it: temperature zero is not deterministic on standard serving, where 13.6 percent of pairwise judgments flip on an identical rerun.9 Reruns average noise, not bias, and no hiring-specific effect size for majority voting was found.
  5. Choice of format: pairwise or pointwise. The formats fail differently, so the choice is a lever: under demographic swaps the pairwise format sat 0.20 from the fair ideal against 0.70 for pointwise scoring.1 Against it: pairwise comparison amplifies wording and tone cues because the judge contrasts them directly,35 carries the first-position advantage, and is the format with the 13.6 percent rerun flips. Neither format is safe alone.
  6. Interventions inside the model: . The one measure that held across realistic settings in the study of realistic context;4 judge bias occupies a low-dimensional activation subspace, and steering it moves scores about ten times more than random directions.36 Against it: it needs the weights, which a school or a vendor using an API model does not have, and it is untested on real applications. This is research, not current practice.
  7. Calibration or fine-tuning on validated labels. Human labels can be budgeted to calibrate several judges at once,37 and a preregistered design re-anchors three model families against blinded human ratings every week.38 Against it: validity is rarely measured because it needs a labelled corpus the model has not seen;11 labels exist only for the admitted;14 and a model trained on past hires cut the Black and Hispanic interview share from 9.4 to 4.2 percent.15

C. Process and people

  1. Human review and escalation. Human checkpoints reduced fabrication beyond guardrails alone;17 in the one end-to-end audit of a deployed hiring pipeline, the human stages corrected some disparities;39 escalation rules with provable agreement guarantees exist for general judging tasks.40 Against it: the same human stages introduced disparities; experienced reviewers shown an algorithmic decile moved 0.42 percentage points;21 and reviewers agree with each other at an alpha of 0.15 to 0.60.12 Approval by a reviewer is not verification.
  2. Re-audit on every model version. The direction of the bias is a property of the release: in the 14-model audit the 2023 model reproduced the pro-White callback gap and every later model showed a null gap or a reversal,6 and alignment training amplified the role of race and gender in another.7 Against it: it is almost never done. Vendor updates are silent, statutory audits are annual and self-reported, and no off-the-shelf harness reruns the test at low cost.

D. Governance

  1. Statutory bias audits with (New York City Local Law 144). The law forces a measurement in a form regulators read: selection-rate ratios and the four-fifths rule. Against it: 18 of 391 covered employers posted an audit;23 the enforcing agency found one non-compliance among 32 companies where the state auditor found at least 17;41 and aggregate selection rates cannot see Problems 1, 2, 4 or 5.
  2. Certification and standards (vendor audits, the , , the EU AI Act, Singapore's ). The EU AI Act makes recruitment AI high-risk, with logging, human oversight and monitoring, obligations now due from December 2027;4258 Colorado enacted a duty-of-care and impact-assessment statute in 2024 and, before it took effect, replaced it in May 2026 with a narrower disclosure regime from January 2027;4360 AI Verify's fairness pillar tests decision consistency under attribute changes.44 Against it: the NIST framework and ISO/IEC 42001 certify that a process exists, not that it caught anything;4546 the most audited vendor's own earlier auditor described its review as not comprehensive;47 and in the hiring literature, 15 to 20 percent of the mitigations proposed for bias and discrimination report an empirical test, and about 60 to 65 percent of all mitigation mentions are proposed without one.48

Why the safeguards do not close the gap

Read together, the fourteen entries share a small number of reasons for falling short, and the reasons are structural rather than a matter of effort.

  1. They act where the failure is visible, not where it lives. Removing the name acts on the file; the demographic signal is in the hobbies and the languages. An instruction acts on the prompt; the decision is made in the activations. The one intervention that held in realistic settings needs the weights, which the deployer does not have.4
  2. They were tested somewhere else. Rubrics, juries and format choice were tested on synthetic input or on general judging tasks. The scoping review of the hiring literature found that 15 to 20 percent of the mitigations proposed for bias and discrimination report an empirical test, that about 60 to 65 percent of all mitigation mentions are proposed without one, and that fairness outcomes were measured about a third as often as efficiency.48 The frontier is not a shortage of proposals but the absence of tests on real screening input.
  3. They reduce noise, not bias. Reruns and juries shrink the run-to-run variance. They leave the shared component of the error in place, and the shared component is what a monoculture spreads: two models that both err agree 60 percent of the time.31
  4. They inherit the standard they are calibrated to. A rubric encodes the committee's criteria; a fine-tuned model learns the committee's labels; both inherit the committee's agreement of 0.15 to 0.60 and the selection of who has an outcome at all.1214
  5. They are done once, and the failure is per release. An audit describes the model that was audited. The sign of the callback gap changed between the 2023 release and every release since.6
  6. They measure the report, not the mechanism. A selection-rate ratio, a log and a human signature are the form the governance measures take. None of them can see a rerun flip, a position effect, or a rationale that does not report the driver.

Measures with positive published evidence exist for Problems 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 and 11, each partial, and for Problem 1 only inside the model. No measure with positive evidence was found for Problems 7, 8, 9, 10, 12 or 13, and for Problems 9 and 10 no measure was found at all. That is the state of the safeguards in September 2026, as far as the published record shows; a measure may exist in a vendor's practice without a published test, and the essay cannot speak to those.

Part four

What the rules require, and what they measure

Governance of automated screening exists in several jurisdictions. What it requires is a report in a fixed form. What it does not require is the measurement that found the failures in Part two.

The duty to report

New York City's has been enforced since 5 July 2023. An employer using an automated employment decision tool must obtain an independent bias audit at least annually, publish a summary, and notify candidates ten business days before the tool is used. The audit reports selection rates and impact ratios by sex, race and ethnicity and their intersections, in the four-fifths form that employment law has used for decades.59 In the first year, of 391 employers that researchers identified as covered, 18 posted an audit.23 The New York State Comptroller then audited the enforcing agency for July 2023 to June 2025: the agency had logged two complaints, reviewed the websites and audits of 32 companies and found one instance of non-compliance, while the Comptroller's auditors found at least 17 instances of potential non-compliance among the same 32, and 75 percent of test calls to the city's complaint line never reached the agency.41

At the federal level, the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission's position has been that Title VII, the Americans with Disabilities Act and the Age Discrimination in Employment Act already reach automated tools through ,61 and its one settled case involved hard age cut-offs in an applicant system, resolved for 365,000 US dollars in 2023.62 Its 2023 technical assistance on assessing adverse impact in software and algorithms was removed from the agency's website on 27 January 2025, following an executive order of 23 January.63 The litigation that matters most is Mobley v. Workday, in which a federal court allowed disparate-impact claims against the vendor to proceed on the theory that the vendor acts as the employer's agent, in July 2024, and conditionally certified a nationwide collective of applicants aged 40 and over in May 2025; in 2026 the court held that the age statute covers applicants, treated the vendor's own bias-testing data as privileged while ordering other records produced, and let proxy-discrimination claims proceed. No trial date has been set.6465

Two states have moved further. Illinois amended its Human Rights Act, in force from 1 January 2026, to make it a civil rights violation for an employer to use artificial intelligence that has a discriminatory effect on a protected class, to bar the use of postal codes as a proxy, and to require notice; the implementing rules on that notice were proposed in May 2026 and withdrawn in June.66 Colorado signed a duty-of-care statute with annual impact assessments in May 2024, delayed it to June 2026, and then, in May 2026, repealed and re-enacted it as a narrower disclosure regime for automated decision-making technology from 1 January 2027. The original framework never took effect.4360

The high-risk category

The European Union's AI Act lists, in its , the uses it classes as high-risk. They include recruitment and the selection of natural persons, access and admission to educational institutions and the evaluation of learning outcomes, creditworthiness, risk assessment and pricing in life and health insurance, eligibility for essential public benefits, law enforcement, migration and border control, and systems "intended to be used by a judicial authority or on their behalf to assist a judicial authority in researching and interpreting facts and the law and in applying the law to a concrete set of facts".42 The list is, almost exactly, the list of places where a language model is now being asked to return a number about a person. The obligations that attach to the category, including logging, human oversight and post-market monitoring, were due to apply from 2 August 2026. On 27 July 2026 a further regulation entered into force that moved that date to 2 December 2027 for the Annex III systems and to 2 August 2028 for high-risk products, on the ground that the harmonised standards were not ready.58

Singapore's instruments are voluntary. AI Verify, launched in 2022 and open-sourced in June 2023, is a self-assessment toolkit whose fairness tests check whether a system makes the same decision when a sensitive attribute is changed;44 the Tripartite Guidelines on Fair Employment Practices operate on a comply-or-explain basis and do not mention automated tools;67 the Workplace Fairness Act, passed in January 2025, will be the first binding employment-discrimination statute to reach decisions assisted by such tools, and has not commenced.68 The United Kingdom's judiciary issued guidance for judicial office holders in December 2023, updated in April and October 2025, which permits some uses of the tools and reminds judges that they are "personally responsible for material which is produced in their name".69

Standards and certificates

The management standards that vendors cite are process standards. The NIST AI Risk Management Framework states that it "does not prescribe risk tolerance" and that its actions "do not constitute a checklist"; it structures an organisation's own process.45 ISO/IEC 42001 is described by the standards body as providing "a practical way of managing AI-related risks and opportunities across an organization" rather than "looking at the details of specific AI applications".46 A certificate under either says that the organisation has a process. It does not say that the process caught a rerun flip, a position effect or a demographic inference. The most audited vendor in the sector announced an external bias audit in 2023 in a release that described its previous review as not comprehensive.47

What the layer measures

Placed against Part two, the governance layer has a definite shape. It creates the obligation to measure and fixes the form of the report: a selection-rate ratio, a log, a human in the loop, a certificate that a process exists. It supplies no measurement that reaches the mechanisms. A selection-rate ratio computed once a year cannot see that the ratio changes sign between two score thresholds, that the same file scores differently on a rerun, that the first-listed file wins, or that the rationale does not report the driver. A log records what the model returned, not whether it would have returned it again. A human signature records that a person approved the output, and the field experiment in Part two shows how little the person may have engaged with it. And the deadlines that would have made the European obligations bite were moved back by sixteen months in the summer of 2026, before any Annex III system had been through them.

The state of the governance, in one paragraph

Every jurisdiction examined here has either a voluntary instrument, a statute that reports selection rates, or a statute whose obligations have been postponed or narrowed before taking effect. None requires the measurements that the published studies used to find the thirteen failures. Enforcement of the one operating statute found one non-compliance where an independent auditor found seventeen. The published record on the safeguards themselves shows 15 to 20 percent of the proposed bias mitigations tested. The gap between what is known about the instrument and what is required of it is the subject of the rest of this essay.

Part five

Beyond hiring: where the same instrument is heading

A screener is one use of a general technique: a language model reads a document about a person and returns a judgment. The technique is now being applied wherever such documents queue up.

The thirteen failures are properties of the technique, not of the hiring setting. A model that rebuilds ethnicity from the languages on a CV rebuilds it from the languages in a loan application. A rationale that does not report the driver of a hiring score does not report the driver of a claim denial. A rerun that flips 13.6 percent of pairwise judgments flips them whether the pair is two candidates or two essays. Three things the cases have in common

The decision is about a named person, made from a document they wrote. The output is a number, a label or a draft that a person is supposed to check. And in every documented case where the checking was examined, the checking was thinner than the policy said: a claim reviewed in 1.2 seconds, an order signed with citations that did not exist, a review written by a model under a rule that required disclosure.

What travels

Each domain inherits the failures that its documents and its decision structure expose. Essay scoring exposes the proxy channel through first language and writing style,7172 the prestige channel through the name of the institution,73 and the adaptation loop through machine-written essays. Lending exposes the gap between the rationale and the driver, in a setting where the regulator has stated that "creditors must be able to specifically explain their reasons for denial. There is no special exemption for artificial intelligence."75 Peer review exposes prompt injection, since a hidden instruction in a manuscript is the same object as a hidden instruction in a CV,87 and the monoculture, since one model reviewing every submission repeats one preference across a whole field.89 Claims processing exposes the human-in-the-loop failure in its starkest documented form: 1.2 seconds of review per denial.76 Courts expose the rationale failure as fabricated citations in signed orders.84 And every domain in the list is one where the person judged has little means of knowing that a model was involved, and less of knowing what it saw.

The earlier systems in the benefits and immigration cases were not language models; they were risk scores built from a handful of fields, one of which was nationality. They are included because they show what happened when an automated score about a person was deployed at national scale with a thin human check and no measurement of its behaviour: a government resigned,79 a royal commission found a scheme "neither fair nor legal",80 and a tool was withdrawn on the eve of a court hearing.82 The language model differs from those systems in one respect that matters here: it reads the whole document, so the proxies it can use are not a handful of fields but everything the person wrote.

Part six

Why the timing matters

Four properties of the instrument make the order of events important: what is required of it now will be harder to require later.

A shared model repeats one error at every gate

The failures in Part two are properties of a model, and the same model is being bought by many institutions at once. In a human market, one reader's rejection is another reader's interview. When one screener serves every employer, an applicant in its blind spot is rejected everywhere, and the welfare loss can exceed the gain from the model being more accurate on average.20 Two models from different vendors are not independent readers either: when both err they agree 60 percent of the time, and the larger models are the more correlated.31 A measurement requirement introduced after a monoculture has formed applies to one model that has already become the default; introduced before, it shapes which models become the default.

The failures are per release, and releases are silent

The sign of the callback gap changed between the 2023 model and every model since,6 and one round of alignment training raised one penalty by 152 percent.7 A vendor updates the model without notice, and a statutory audit is annual and self-reported. Every year in which the rules do not require a per-release measurement is a year of decisions made under a bias whose direction nobody recorded.

The applicant pool adapts, and does not adapt back

The share of personal statements flagged as machine-assisted rose from 2.6 to 22.5 percent in one year;56 the correlation between cover-letter quality and callbacks fell by 51 percent when a writing tool became available;18 the writing tool and the screener favour each other.57 Each cycle of screening teaches the next cohort what the screener rewards. A standard introduced later meets a pool that has already been shaped, and the signal it would have measured is partly gone.

The rules being written now fix the form of the report for years

New York City fixed the four-fifths selection-rate ratio as the form of its audit in 2021, and every audit since has taken that form.59 The European harmonised standards that will define what "tested" means for a high-risk system are being drafted now, against a deadline that was moved to December 2027;58 Colorado's framework was narrowed to disclosure before it took effect;60 Singapore's binding statute has been passed and has not commenced.68 The technical content of these instruments, which measurements they require, at which cadence, on what input, is being decided in 2026 and 2027, and the published record shows that the measurements that find the failures are not the measurements the instruments currently name.

This essay does not argue that the tools should not be used, and it does not claim that the human readers they replace were reliable; Part two shows they were not. It argues something narrower: the instrument is being deployed before the measurements that would characterise it exist in the rules that govern it, and the moment to require those measurements is before the deployments become the default. Whether the requirement is proportionate in a given setting is a judgment the essay leaves to the people who make it.

Glossary

Language model as a judge

Using a language model to grade or rank something on behalf of a person: an essay, an answer, an application. The model receives the item and a description of the criteria, and returns a score, a rank or a choice, often with a written justification. The technique was developed to evaluate other models' outputs at scale and carries measured biases there: for the item in the first position, for longer items, and for items written by the judge's own model family. A screener is this technique applied to a person's file.

Glossary

Macro F1

A score for a classifier from 0 to 1. For each class, the F1 score is the harmonic mean of precision (the share of predicted members of the class that belong to it) and recall (the share of true members that were found). Macro F1 averages the per-class scores with equal weight, so a rare class counts as much as a common one. A macro F1 of 0.97 for ethnicity means the model recovered nearly every applicant's ethnicity across the groups, not only the largest.

Glossary

Area under the curve

The probability that a classifier ranks a randomly chosen positive example above a randomly chosen negative one. A value of 0.5 is chance; 1.0 is perfect separation. An area of 0.996 for gender from the text embeddings of real applications means the representation the model builds of the file separates the two groups almost completely, whether or not the model is asked about gender.

Glossary

Adverse-impact ratio and the four-fifths rule

The selection rate of one group divided by the selection rate of the group with the highest rate. Under the four-fifths rule used in United States employment practice since 1978, a ratio below 0.8 is taken as evidence of adverse impact. New York City's audit law reports these ratios by sex, race and ethnicity and their intersections. The ratio depends on where the score threshold is placed; in one audit it changed sign between thresholds 4 and 5. It summarises outcomes across a group and cannot see whether an individual file would have scored differently on a rerun or in a different position.

Glossary

Krippendorff's alpha

A measure of agreement between raters that corrects for the agreement expected by chance. A value of 1.0 means the raters gave every item the same code; 0 means their agreement is what chance would produce; negative values mean systematic disagreement. A common rule of thumb treats 0.67 as the floor for tentative conclusions and 0.80 for reliable ones. Admissions officers coding personal qualities reached 0.15 to 0.60, which sets a ceiling on how well any model can agree with them.

Glossary

Logit

The natural logarithm of the odds. A shift of 1.74 logits multiplies the odds by about 5.7. In the residency study, a rater's odds of giving a favourable score were that much higher for an applicant of the rater's own gender, holding the applicant's file constant.

Glossary

Selective labels

The situation in which an outcome is observed only for the people who passed a decision: whether a student thrived is known only for the admitted, whether a borrower repaid only for those given the loan. A model cannot be checked on the people it would have accepted and the human readers rejected, because they have no outcome. Naive comparisons that ignore this can show a model beating the human readers when it does not.

Glossary

Range restriction

The change in the relationship between two variables that occurs when a sample is selected on a combination of them. Two scores that are unrelated in the applicant pool become negatively related among the admitted when admission depends on their sum, because a low value on one had to be compensated by a high value on the other. The worked example on this page uses two uniform scores and a threshold of 14; the correlation among the admitted is exactly negative 0.50.

Glossary

Temperature zero and batch invariance

Temperature controls how much randomness the sampler adds when the model chooses each token; at zero it takes the most probable token every time. The output can still differ between runs because the arithmetic is not identical between runs: floating-point addition is not associative, and the serving kernels change the order of additions with the size of the batch a request happens to share, which follows the server's load. Kernels that keep the same order regardless of batch size are called batch-invariant; with them, 1,000 identical requests produced 1,000 identical outputs, against 80 distinct outputs without them.

Glossary

Pairwise and pointwise

Two ways to ask a judge. Pointwise: score this file alone on a fixed scale. Pairwise: here are two files, which is stronger. The two formats fail differently. Pointwise scoring gave identical files different scores; pairwise choice carried a first-position advantage and amplified wording cues because the judge contrasts the two texts directly. Under demographic swaps the pairwise format sat 0.20 from the fair ideal against 0.70 for pointwise.

Glossary

Prompt injection

Text placed in a document so that a model reading the document treats it as an instruction: for instance, white text on a white background telling the screener to rate the candidate highly. A human reader does not see it; the model does. Injections have been found in a corpus of about 200,000 real resumes, and detection tools exist for hidden text in structured documents.

Glossary

Algorithmic monoculture

The situation in which many decision makers rely on the same model, so that its errors are made everywhere at once. The term comes from a 2021 analysis showing that a shared algorithm can lower overall welfare even when it is more accurate than the independent human judgments it replaces, because the independence of those judgments had value of its own. In hiring, one vendor's shared screener has been shown to route a measurable share of applications to positions with adverse impact.

Glossary

Disparate impact

In United States employment law, a practice that is neutral on its face but falls more heavily on a protected group, which the employer must then justify as job-related and consistent with business necessity. It is the doctrine under which an automated screener can be challenged without proof that anyone intended to discriminate, and the theory on which the claims in Mobley v. Workday proceed.

Glossary

Annex III of the EU AI Act

The list of uses that Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 classes as high-risk: biometrics, critical infrastructure, education and vocational training, employment and workers' management, access to essential private and public services (including credit and life and health insurance), law enforcement, migration and border control, and the administration of justice and democratic processes. Providers of a system in the list must meet obligations on risk management, data governance, logging, human oversight and post-market monitoring. The date from which those obligations apply was moved from 2 August 2026 to 2 December 2027 by Regulation (EU) 2026/1744.

Glossary

New York City Local Law 144

The first statute anywhere to require a bias audit of automated employment decision tools, enacted in 2021 and enforced from 5 July 2023. The audit is an independent, annual calculation of selection rates and impact ratios by sex, race and ethnicity; a summary must be published and candidates notified ten business days before use. Researchers found 18 of 391 covered employers had posted an audit, and the State Comptroller found enforcement had identified one non-compliance where its own auditors found at least 17.

Glossary

AI Verify

Singapore's voluntary testing framework and toolkit for AI governance, released as a minimum viable product in 2022 and open-sourced through the AI Verify Foundation in June 2023. It combines a process checklist with technical tests; its fairness test checks whether a system makes the same decision when a sensitive attribute is changed. It is a self-assessment; it does not certify a system and no statute requires it.

Glossary

NIST AI Risk Management Framework

A voluntary framework published by the United States National Institute of Standards and Technology in January 2023 to help organisations structure how they govern, map, measure and manage AI risk. Its own text states that it does not prescribe risk tolerance and that its actions do not constitute a checklist. It describes a process an organisation can adopt; it does not test a system.

Glossary

ISO/IEC 42001

An international management-system standard for artificial intelligence, published in December 2023, in the family of ISO 9001 for quality and ISO 27001 for information security. Certification audits an organisation's policies, roles and procedures for AI, which the standards body describes as managing risks across an organisation rather than looking at the details of specific applications. A certificate says the management system exists.

Glossary

Alignment training

The stage of training in which a model is adjusted, usually with human feedback, to follow instructions and to avoid outputs its developer considers harmful. It changes what the model says. The studies on this page found that it can leave the model's internal representation of a person's group intact while removing that group from the text of the rationale, and that it can raise one demographic penalty while lowering another.

Glossary

Probes, steering and activations

Activations are the numbers a model computes internally as it reads a text. A probe is a small classifier trained on those numbers to detect a concept, such as the applicant's gender, whether or not the model's text mentions it. Steering adds or removes a direction in the activations to change the model's behaviour. These methods need access to the weights, which a deployer using a vendor's API does not have, and they have not been tested on real applications.

Glossary

Rubric decomposition

Instead of asking the model for one overall score, the model is asked to extract specific features (years of experience, degree, a named skill) and a fixed rule combines them. It reduced the share of judgments that flipped when an irrelevant detail was changed, by a median of 49 percent across 11 models in one study. The rubric encodes whoever wrote it, so the noise in the human standard is inherited rather than removed.

Glossary

Strict-dominance pair

A test pair of two files that are identical except that one is better on a single attribute, such as one more year of relevant experience. A correct judge should always prefer the dominant file. Accuracy on such pairs measures whether the judge can resolve a small, unambiguous difference; in one study 93 percent of the errors were abstentions, where the judge declined to choose.

Glossary

Jury of models

Several models judge the same item and their scores are averaged or their votes combined. Averaging removes the part of the error on which the members disagree; it leaves the part they share. Because models from different vendors give the same wrong answer 60 percent of the time when both err, a jury reduces noise more than it reduces bias, and one member that fails systematically can pull the consensus arbitrarily far.
