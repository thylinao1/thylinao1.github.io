ARC White-Box Estimation Challenge 2026  /  Phase 1

Estimating a random network without running it

You are handed the weights of a neural network that nobody trained. Predict what its neurons will output on average, under random input, without being allowed to feed it enough inputs to find out. This page explains the problem from zero, the method I submitted, and what the rest of the field discovered.

01  /  The problem

A network with random weights still has a definite answer

Nothing here is learned. The weights are drawn from a fixed distribution and left alone. The question is what the network does on average, and whether you can work that out faster than by trying it.

Take a plain multilayer perceptron with activations and no bias terms. Feed it a random input drawn from a standard Gaussian. Each layer multiplies by a weight matrix and then clips everything negative to zero. Repeat thirty-two times.

h0 = X, X ~ N(0, In) hl = ReLU(Wl hl-1) for l = 1 .. L

Every individual run gives a different answer, because the input is random. But the average over all possible inputs is a fixed number for each neuron. That number is what you have to predict:

Yhat[l, i] ≈ EX ~ N(0, I) [ hli(X) ]

In Phase 1 the network has L = 32 hidden layers of width n = 256, so the answer is a 32 by 256 matrix. Weights use He-Gaussian initialisation with variance 2/n, stored as float32, and there are no biases at all. Only the final row, layer 32, is scored for the prize. The other rows are a secondary diagnostic.

The handicap that makes it a competition. The answer key was produced by brute-force Monte Carlo at roughly 4.24e15 floating point operations per network. You are given 2.72e11. That is about one fifteen-thousandth of the budget that produced the target you are being compared against.

So the benchmark is not really asking you to compute an average. It is asking whether understanding the structure of a random network lets you beat brute force at a fifteen-thousand-to-one disadvantage. That framing is the whole design of the thing, and it is why the scoring rule matters more than the mathematics.

02  /  The obvious method

Sampling works, and it is charged by the sample

Draw inputs, run them through, average the results. It converges. The only question is what it costs, and the competition answers that question very precisely.

The straightforward estimator is . Draw N random inputs, push all of them through the network, and average the post-ReLU values at the final layer. The error of that average falls as 1/N, so the root mean squared error falls as one over the square root of N. Getting one more digit of accuracy costs a hundred times more samples.

There is a smarter version. replaces random draws with a carefully constructed point set that covers the space more evenly than randomness manages. The classic construction is a , and randomising it by keeps the estimator unbiased while preserving the even coverage. On smooth problems this converges considerably faster than 1/N.

A number worth remembering. The grader reports a constant called sampling_mse equal to 6.469470189211361e-07. That is what an ordinary independent-draw Monte Carlo estimator scores when it spends the entire budget. Everything on the leaderboard is measured against that reference.

03  /  The metric

Accuracy is multiplied by what it cost you

This is the rule that decides everything else. Read it slowly, because almost every wrong turn in this competition comes from reading it quickly.

For each network m, the score is the final-layer mean squared error multiplied by the fraction of the compute budget you consumed:

sm = MSEfinal,m × max(0.1, Cm / Bm)

The overall score is the mean of sm over the networks. Lower is better. Three pieces need unpacking.

C, the compute you actually spent

Cm = Fm + λ × Rm, λ = 1e11 FLOPs per second

F is the analytic count that the metering library charges you for every arithmetic operation you issue. R is residual wall-clock time, meaning time spent in your own Python between metered calls. One second of un-metered Python costs the same as a hundred billion metered operations. Several participants measured that the residual charge behaves as a tax per function call rather than per unit of arithmetic, which makes the number of calls a design variable in its own right.

B, the budget

2.72e11 FLOPs per network in Phase 1. For scale, one honest dense forward pass of a single input through the whole 32-layer network costs about 4.19e6 FLOPs, so the budget buys roughly sixty-five thousand honest forward passes.

The floor at 0.1, and the cliff at 1.0

The multiplier is max(0.1, C/B). Spending less than ten percent of the budget does not reduce the multiplier any further, so roughly 2.72e10 FLOPs are effectively free. Above the floor the multiplier is not capped at one. The grader's own source describes it as "uncapped above". And going over budget entirely is a cliff rather than a slope: when C > B the grader zeroes your predictions for that network.

This cliff was not always enforced. Several participants documented that the combined-budget check did not fire on the evaluator until around 9 August 2026, and that older submissions were then re-graded retroactively. One submission that had been reading in the region of 4e-8 per network became an aggregate 0.682 after the patch, with 49 of the public networks failed. Anyone reading old leaderboard snapshots should know that they were produced under a different rule.

If you are reading the challenge page, its numbers disagree with the grader's. At the time of writing, the public challenge page describes the score with a floor of max(0.5, C/B), eight hidden layers, and a budget around 3.4e10. Every number on this page instead follows the installed grader and starter kit, which give a floor of 0.1, thirty-two hidden layers, and 2.72e11. My own graded submission records a multiplier of 0.4401, which is below 0.5 and therefore could not exist under the page's stated floor, so the grader is the operative source. This looks like the page carrying warm-up-round text that was never updated. Worth knowing before you calibrate anything against it.

04  /  The invariance

For a sampler, drawing more samples changes nothing

This is the single most useful thing to understand about the competition, and it takes two lines of algebra.

A sampler has error that falls as one over the sample count, and a price that rises linearly with it:

MSE = a / N Error falls as one over the sample count. a is the variance produced per sample, and it is the only thing in this line you control.
C = p × N Cost rises in step with the sample count. p is the billed price of drawing one sample and pushing it through the network.

Substitute both into the score:

s = (a / N) × (p N / B) = a × p / B

The N cancels. Above the floor, the score of a sampler does not depend on how many samples it draws. Drawing more buys accuracy and pays for it at exactly the rate the metric charges. You can move along that line in either direction and arrive at the same number.

Move the slider in Figure 4 and watch it happen. The error falls, the cost rises, and the product sits still.

Two honest caveats

The exponent is not exactly one. Measured on my own split, the empirical scaling exponent is 1.0653: error 2.0156e-06 at N = 13,312 falling to 8.4253e-08 at N = 262,144, a factor of 23.92 over a sample ratio of 19.69. Because the exponent sits slightly above one, raising N is very slightly score-positive rather than exactly neutral, which is why my build spends every row it can afford.

Somebody else measured the same thing independently. Participant violeta ran a four-point paired ladder and reported the product of error and effective compute as 8.180e4 / 7.554e4 / 8.350e4 / 7.448e4 FLOPs across four batch sizes. That product is the quantity the algebra says is conserved, and it is conserved to within about ten percent across an eightfold change in sample count.

What this rules out

Brute force is mathematically dead, and you can price the death exactly. Reaching a raw error of 8.40e-08 by sampling alone would need N around 262,900, which drives budget consumption to 1.797 and the score to 1.509e-07. That is worse than the estimator I already had, while also being over budget. No amount of patience gets you there.

What is left is exactly two quantities. Lower a, the variance produced per sample, or lower p, the price billed per sample. Everything interesting anyone did in this competition is one of those two things.

05  /  The analytic route

The baseline everybody starts from

If the input to a neuron were exactly Gaussian, the expected value of its ReLU would have a closed form. You could carry a Gaussian description forward layer by layer and never sample at all.

That idea is called , and the starter kit ships a working version of it. Here is the loop, from examples/02_mean_propagation.py:

mu = zeros(width) # input mean, exact var = ones(width) # input variance, exact for w in mlp.weights: mu_pre = w.T @ mu # linear layers move the mean exactly var_pre = (w * w).T @ var # the approximation lives here sigma = sqrt(maximum(var_pre, 1e-12)) alpha = mu_pre / sigma # distance from zero, in own sigmas mu = mu_pre * Phi(alpha) + sigma * phi(alpha) # E[ReLU(Z)], exact for Gaussian Z ez2 = (mu_pre**2 + var_pre) * Phi(alpha) + mu_pre * sigma * phi(alpha) var = maximum(ez2 - mu * mu, 0.0) rows.append(mu)

Reading it line by line

The first two lines are exact. A standard Gaussian input has mean zero and variance one in every coordinate. The line mu_pre = w.T @ mu is also exact, because a linear map moves a mean exactly.

The approximation enters at var_pre = (w*w).T @ var. The true pre-activation variance is the diagonal of W⁠T Cov W, which needs the entire covariance matrix. Squaring the weights and pushing only the diagonal through gives the same answer only if the incoming neurons are uncorrelated. After the first layer they are not.

The line that computes mu is the exact expectation of ReLU(Z) for a Gaussian Z with that mean and standard deviation, where Phi is the normal cumulative distribution function and phi its density. That formula is not the weak point. All of the error comes from the assumption that the thing entering the ReLU was Gaussian in the first place.

Plain Monte Carlo at full budget is about 1,500 times more accurate than the diagonal closure, and about 130 times more accurate than full covariance propagation. The closure's only advantage is that it is far cheaper, which under this metric is a real advantage, but it is not an accuracy advantage.

06  /  Why it breaks

Thirty-two layers destroy the assumption

The closure is not slightly wrong at depth 32. Establishing exactly where it goes wrong took most of my campaign, and the answer changed what I built.

Split the error into two parts. Readout error is what you lose in the final step, turning the last layer's pre-activation distribution into an expected ReLU. Propagation error is what you lose in the preceding 31 steps, carrying the distribution forward.

You can measure them separately by cheating in a controlled way. Hand the estimator the true quantity it would otherwise approximate, leave everything else running, and see what error remains. That gives an upper bound on every possible improvement that works through that quantity, including ideas nobody has thought of yet. Participant trim_qewas published the same protocol independently and used it to close 54 families of estimators without building any of them.

Oracle experimentResidual errorReading

Put those side by side. Perfect readout given perfect moments reaches 2.2e-08. Perfect marginals at every single layer, with only the correlation structure left approximate, cannot get below 7.07e-07. The gap between them is the dependence structure the closure discards, and it is roughly thirty times larger than everything the readout could ever contribute. The readout is about 3 percent of the chain error.

Participant amalgonim reached the same conclusion by a different route, with a permutation-shuffle oracle: give the estimator all 256 exact marginal distributions but force it to assume independence, and the error lands at 9.2643e-07. Exact marginals with no dependence information are worth almost nothing. The dependence is the problem.

What actually happens to the correlations

They do not decay. They saturate near one. Iterating the ReLU angle map

c → ( sqrt(1 - c²) + (π - arccos c) · c ) / π

from the most extreme possible starting point, two exactly opposite inputs at c = -1, gives 0 after one layer, then 0.318 at layer 2, 0.605 at layer 4, 0.809 at layer 8, 0.923 at layer 16, and 0.972 at layer 31. Measured directly, the cosine between h31(x) and h31(-x) is 0.982 against that theoretical 0.972.

Two inputs as different as two inputs can possibly be end up 98 percent aligned by the layer that gets scored. Team SOX measured the consequence on this exact architecture class: 73 dead neurons, 106 kink neurons, 77 always-on neurons, at an effective rank of 2.2. A 256-dimensional activation vector that is effectively living on a two-dimensional object.

One more measurement worth knowing, from participant Cipo, who is one of the few who predicted every layer rather than only the scored one. Their error profile grows from 2.940e-09 at layer 0 to a peak of 6.709e-05 at layer 9, decays to 2.217e-05 by layer 30, and then the scored layer comes back down to 1.964e-07. The interior is where the damage happens, and the scored layer is anomalously easy compared to the layers before it.

The design conclusion follows directly. If roughly 99 percent of the achievable error lives in propagating a 256-dimensional joint distribution through 32 ReLUs, and every cheap surrogate for that propagation is measured to fail, then the budget is better spent on samples, which propagate the true distribution exactly, one draw at a time. That is the decision my estimator embodies.

07  /  What I submitted

A quasi-Monte Carlo sampler, and machinery to make it cheap

The estimator itself is simple to state. Everything else in the build exists to lower the billed price of one sample, because the algebra in section 4 says that is one of only two quantities that can move the score.

The estimator draws N = 98,304 rows of standard Gaussian input, pushes all of them through the 32 layers exactly in float32, and returns the mean of the final layer's post-ReLU columns. No closure, no correction, no learned component in the scored path.

The point set

The 98,304 rows are six independently scrambled Sobol nets of 214 points each in 256 dimensions, mapped to Gaussians through the inverse normal distribution function with a clip at 1e-7. Six independent scrambles rather than one long stream, because independent replicates give an error estimate for free. A paired measurement made afterwards put the convergence exponent at 0.998 for the six-block construction against 0.994 for a single scrambled net and 0.975 for plain independent draws, which says the split costs nothing in rate rather than that it wins.

The point set ships as a precomputed float16 asset of exactly 48.00 MiB, against a 50 MiB package cap. It is loaded and upcast to float32 inside setup(), which runs outside any budget context and is therefore charged to nothing. The float16 round trip costs a measured error ratio of 1.000662, which is inside noise.

The cost machinery

An honest dense forward pass bills about 4.19e6 FLOPs per sample. The shipped build pays about 1.16e6. About half of that gap is the cost-model defect described below rather than a real saving, so the legitimate stack is roughly 1.9 times. It is assembled from these pieces:

One item is not a legitimate discount, and I am naming it. The build padded its arrays so that each contraction exceeded a rank threshold in the metering library, which routed it to a shape-only estimate that billed 0.500978 times the honest charge. That is a cost-model defect, not a mathematical saving. I reported it to the organizers, and when offered the choice between having the model patched with my rank possibly falling, or leaving the submission as graded and deferring to review, I elected to have it patched. The honest number for my estimator is the one in the next section, and it is roughly 1.8 times worse than the one currently on the public board.

What the estimator is not

It is not unbiased, and the write-up says so. Two levers move the estimand. Pilot-predicted pruning can classify a live neuron as dead, which was gated against a threshold and measured at worst at 86.3 percent of that threshold on the hardest public network, with held-out bias statistically indistinguishable from zero. Certified always-on columns were mis-certified on 60 of 2261 columns across 40 networks, always in one direction, contributing +8.9e-06 relative error. Anyone quoting this build should quote those numbers with it.

09  /  The measurement

A control variate that works on random draws and returns nothing on a net

This is the one piece of original work in my Phase 1 write-up. It is a negative result, and the reason it is worth reporting is that the null is measured against a positive control in the same script.

A is the standard way to reduce the variance of a sampler. Build a quantity that correlates with what you are integrating and whose true mean you already know, then subtract a fitted multiple of its error. My graded estimator spends its whole budget on the point set and nothing on variance reduction downstream, so the obvious question is whether adding a control on top would have paid.

The experiment runs both arms in one script, on the same integrand, with the same dictionary and the same fitted coefficients. The only thing that changes is the point set underneath.

ArmVariance ratio with the controlReading
Independent draws0.6664 The control removes 33 percent of the variance. It works.
Scrambled Sobol net1.0052 Nothing the instrument can resolve, against a resolution limit near 5 percent.

The independent arm landing exactly where the run pre-registered it is what makes the null on the net readable. A null with no positive control in the same script is indistinguishable from a broken instrument.

The explanation is an accounting one. The control targets about a third of the variance. The point set has already removed about 69 percent of it, and the third the control is reaching for sits inside that. Measured on the same integrand, the carrier's own gain over independent draws is 3.19×, 3.13× and 3.24×. There is nothing left in that band for the control to take.

What is classical here and what is not

The vanishing of scramble variance for low-order Walsh functions is not mine. It is Owen's gain-coefficient theorem from 1998, which says the coefficients vanish when the order is below a threshold set by the net's parameters. That a digital net equidistributes every dyadic box of the right volume is the definition of the object, not a measurement.

A caveat I have to state. Owen's decomposition is proved for nested uniform scrambling. My rig uses a linear matrix scramble plus a digital shift. That preserves the equidistribution which forces the low-order means to vanish, but it is not the randomisation the variance theorem is stated for, so the theorem is motivation here rather than proof.

What I claim as mine is narrower: that a fixed, input-anchored control returns no measurable reduction on a scrambled digital net while the same control with the same coefficients returns a third of the variance on independent draws, measured in one script across both arms. Two other participants reached related nulls by different routes, attributing them to shallow controls decorrelating with depth and to estimation noise respectively. For a fixed input-anchored dictionary neither explanation is needed, because the low-order part of the control is empty before any network is involved.

The price a future method has to pay

Both inputs to this are public and I did not find the product stated anywhere, so it is worth writing down. Propagating the readout gain through the published spread of neuron activity: a total final-layer error of 1e-8 requires the terminal mean to be accurate to about 1.4e-4 RMS after the gain, which is 1e-4 RMS on the post-ReLU mean itself.

What that costs

By direct sampling, 1e-8 needs roughly 0.8e7 to 1.7e7 rows at my own implied per-row variance, and about 1.9e7 rows at another participant's published constant. The per-network budget buys about 6.5e4 dense rows, or about 1.2e5 at the billed cost per row my de-padded build achieves. That is two orders of magnitude short.

By propagation instead, it needs a closure roughly a hundred times better than the Gaussian terminal closure, which floors at 9.33e-07 given exact mean and standard deviation, and it sits below the exact-moment Edgeworth readout floor of 1.5e-08 to 2.2e-08 outright.

Another participant asked publicly what compact statistic could transport signed cross-neuron dependence through 32 ReLU layers. I do not have that statistic. What I have is its price, and the price says that neither of the two routes the field has tried can pay it.

10  /  Dead ends

What I measured and had to abandon

These are the things I tried that did not work, with the number that killed each one. A measured dead end is worth more to a newcomer than a technique that worked, because it is the part nobody writes down.

IdeaWhat killed it
Readout-side correctors. Improve the last step, where the final pre-activation distribution becomes an expected ReLU. Plateaued at 2.5e-05. Section 6 explains why: the readout is about 3 percent of the chain error, so perfecting it cannot matter.
Per-layer propagation correctors. Learn or derive a correction applied after every layer. Failed its oracle-strength kill line by a factor of five, on two independent instruments. The oracle version, given true marginals at every layer, only reaches 2.11e-06.
Correcting only the standard deviation. The mean is unpredictable but sigma looked learnable. Actively harmful. The closure's mean error and sigma error partially cancel, so fixing one breaks the cancellation: 2.75e-04 against an uncorrected 6.9e-05, about four times worse.
Antithetic sampling. Pair every draw with its negation. Capped at 1.10× by the arc-cosine map itself. I attribute the cap to the arc-cosine map; another participant reports a sharper spectral account of it. Note that this result is contested, and the field guide in section 11 flags the disagreement.
Input-anchored control variates. Build a control from the input coordinates, fit its coefficients, subtract. Removes 33 percent of the variance on independent draws and nothing measurable on a scrambled net: variance ratio 1.0052 against a resolution limit near 5 percent. The net had already removed what the control was reaching for.
Rotations of the point set, and Owen scrambling variants. Dead on randomised QMC. Rotation-robust sets could not beat plain Sobol; scrambling differences sat inside measurement noise.
A cross-network trained asset. Train one point set or corrector on a family of networks and reuse it. Flat at 1.015× even with eight times more training data. The gain does not transfer across networks.
A third level of Strassen recursion. Measured +1.28% billed cost, so it made things worse. Another participant independently measured deeper recursion regressing metered compute by 2.383 percent in all three paired repeats.
Adaptive sample count per network. Spend more rows on harder networks. Score-negative and shipped inert. A top-up row costs 2.113e6 against the main block's marginal 1.4e6, so the extra accuracy does not pay for itself. Every network runs the same N.

The pattern in all of them

Every one of these was aimed at the wrong one percent. Section 6 established that propagation, not readout, carries almost all of the achievable error. The corrector family, the readout family and the Edgeworth family all operate on the readout. They were careful, they were measured, and they were pointed at a part of the problem that could not pay.

The one lever that survived every kill is variance per sample at fixed billed cost. It has an existence proof: a rival submission dropped its raw error by 2.31 times while holding billed FLOPs, multiplier, wall-clock, backend and per-network allocation identical to its own control. Whatever they did is real, and I did not find it.

The pair integral that a covariance-propagating estimator needs at every layer has a classical series expansion. Using the series instead of numerical quadrature changes the cost by three orders of magnitude.

If you are picking this up from zero

The order that would have saved me the most time:

1. Work through the metric before writing an estimator

Section 4 is worth doing on paper rather than reading. A sampler's score is a × p / B, and it does not depend on the sample count. If that is not settled in advance, it is easy to spend a long time adjusting a parameter that the algebra says cannot change the result.

2. Build the measurement setup before the estimator

Most of the published write-ups contain a section about a measurement that later turned out to be wrong. Rotation noise moves a single network's error by a factor of several. The answer key has its own floor near 5e-11. The public 50 networks are an unusually easy draw, at roughly the 0.19th percentile of ordinary draws. A three-replicate decomposition reversed at least one participant's earlier recorded verdict. It is worth deciding how a 3 percent effect will be distinguished from noise before there is a 3 percent effect to discuss.

3. Use oracle substitution before building anything

The protocol described in the field guide is inexpensive and rules out a great deal of work. Replace an internal quantity with its true value and measure what error remains. If that bound sits above the target, every method in the family is ruled out and none of them needs to be written. It is a faster way to reach a negative result than implementing the methods one at a time.

4. Read the cost model as carefully as the mathematics

About a third of the published techniques concern billing rather than estimation. Wall time is charged per call. Float64 costs double, and the statistics helpers return it without being asked. Symmetric contractions are priced at half. Exceeding the budget discards the prediction. None of this appears in the paper the competition is based on, and all of it enters the score.

A summary of the position

Sampling propagates the true distribution exactly and is charged linearly for doing so. Analytic propagation is close to free but discards the dependence structure, and at depth 32 the dependence structure accounts for almost the entire answer. Each of the stronger entries is a particular measured compromise between those two facts, and the more interesting ideas are the ones that obtain dependence information without paying for it one sample at a time.

The leading entry on the public board scores 7.5 times better than my honest submission, and does it while spending slightly more compute rather than less. As far as I can tell from the public record, the mechanism has not been explained. That question appears to be open.

Concept

ReLU

The rectified linear unit is the function max(x, 0). It passes positive values through unchanged and sets everything negative to zero. It is the most common activation function in modern networks because it is cheap and because it does not saturate for large positive inputs.

Two properties matter for this problem. First, it is positively homogeneous: ReLU(t·x) = t·ReLU(x) for any positive t. In a network with no bias terms this means scaling the input scales every activation by the same factor, which is what makes the radial-angular split in the field guide exact.

Second, it is not smooth. The kink at zero is where all the difficulty lives. A neuron whose pre-activation sits far from zero is either always on, in which case it behaves linearly, or always off, in which case it contributes nothing. Only neurons near the kink are genuinely hard, and one participant measured that about 66 of them carry nearly the whole error.

A worked case. Suppose a neuron's pre-activation over random inputs has mean 1.5 and standard deviation 1. Its standardised distance from the kink is 1.5, so it is on for about 93 percent of inputs and the ReLU barely bites: its expected output is close to its expected input. Now suppose the mean is 0 instead. It is on for exactly half of inputs, the clipping removes half the distribution, and the expected output is sigma/sqrt(2π) ≈ 0.399 rather than 0. The second neuron is the hard one, and a method that gets its distribution slightly wrong pays for it directly.

Concept

Monte Carlo

Estimating an average by drawing random samples and averaging them. If you want E[f(X)], draw N independent copies of X, compute f on each, and take the mean. The estimate is unbiased, and its variance falls as 1/N, so the typical error falls as 1/√N.

That square root is the whole problem. One extra decimal digit of accuracy costs a hundred times more samples, and this holds regardless of how many dimensions the problem has, which is why Monte Carlo is the default in high dimensions and also why it is slow.

In this competition the ordinary Monte Carlo reference at full budget scores 6.469470189211361e-07, a constant the grader ships in every payload. The leaderboard's own "vs sampling" column is not quite that number: it reconciles to roughly 7.0e-7, about eight percent higher.

A worked case. Say a single sample has variance 1 and you want the mean to within 0.001. You need N ≈ 1/0.001² = 1e6 samples. To reach 0.0001 you need 1e8. The competition budget buys roughly 6.5e4 full forward passes per network, which fixes the accuracy plain sampling can reach before any cleverness is applied. That is why every strong entry is trying to cut the constant in front of the rate rather than the rate itself.

Concept

Quasi-Monte Carlo

The same averaging, but with points chosen deliberately rather than at random. Random points clump: in any finite sample some regions get too many and some too few, purely by chance. A low-discrepancy point set is constructed so that every region gets close to its fair share, at every scale simultaneously.

For smooth enough integrands the error of a quasi-Monte Carlo average can fall close to 1/N rather than 1/√N, which for large N is an enormous difference. The catch is that the guarantee degrades with dimension, and this problem has 256 dimensions at the input and a much larger effective dimension by the time you are deep in the network.

My own measurement on this task found a convergence exponent of about 1.065 on the squared error, which is close to the Monte Carlo rate rather than the ideal quasi-Monte Carlo rate. Another participant independently measured about 1.07. That is itself a finding: by layer 30 the structure of the input point set has largely been destroyed, so the input geometry is no longer where the leverage is.

Where the guarantee comes from, and where it goes. The classical bound is Koksma-Hlawka: the integration error is at most the variation of the integrand times the discrepancy of the point set. Discrepancy for a good net falls like (log N)^s / N in s dimensions, which beats 1/sqrt(N) handsomely when s is small. At s = 256 the logarithmic factor is enormous, so the bound is useless as a guarantee, and whether quasi-Monte Carlo helps becomes an empirical question about the particular integrand. On this problem the measured answer is that it helps at the start and is largely gone by layer 30.

Concept

Sobol sequences

The best known construction of a low-discrepancy point set. A Sobol sequence is a digital net in base 2, built from a set of direction numbers so that the binary representation of each coordinate is a linear function, over the two-element field, of the binary representation of the point's index.

The defining property is stated in terms of dyadic boxes. Split the unit cube by repeatedly halving along each axis. For a good digital net, every such box of volume 2-m contains exactly its fair share of the first 2m points. Not approximately, exactly. That is what "low discrepancy" means concretely, and it is why the counts in Figure 7 are flat for the Sobol panel and ragged for the random one.

My build uses six independent scrambled Sobol nets of 214 points each, in 256 dimensions, mapped to Gaussians through the inverse normal distribution function.

Why the dyadic property matters here. A neuron's answer depends on which side of its kink each sample lands. Splitting the cube evenly at every scale is close to saying that every such split is sampled in proportion, which is exactly the structure the ReLU cares about. That is the intuition for why a net helps at all. What the measurements in section 4 show is that by layer 30 the effective dimension has grown far past what the construction can control, and the advantage has mostly been spent.

Concept

Scrambling

A raw Sobol net is deterministic, which means it has a bias you cannot see and cannot estimate. Scrambling applies a random but structure-preserving permutation to the digits of every coordinate. The result is a point set where each individual point is uniformly distributed, so the average is unbiased, while the even coverage of the original net survives intact.

This gives you the best of both. You keep the fast convergence, and because each scramble is random you can run several independently and use the spread between them as an honest error bar. That is why my build ships six independent scrambles rather than one long stream.

The theory here is classical. The key result is Owen's gain-coefficient theorem from 1998, which says the scramble variance vanishes exactly for a large class of basis functions, with the surviving error living on a specific dual set. It is worth knowing that this is textbook material rather than anything discovered in this competition.

Concept

Moment closure

A general technique for propagating a distribution through a chain of operations when you cannot carry the whole distribution. You pick a small set of summary statistics, usually the first two or three moments, push those through each step, and at every step re-fit a distribution of a chosen shape that matches them. Everything the summary does not capture is discarded.

Gaussian closure picks the mean and variance and re-fits a Gaussian. It is exact for the linear part of a layer, because a linear map moves means and covariances exactly. It is inexact for the ReLU, because the output of a ReLU is not Gaussian: it has an atom of probability sitting at exactly zero and a skewed positive tail.

Closure is not wrong because the formulas are wrong. Every formula in the starter kit baseline is exact given its assumption. It is wrong because the assumption stops being true after the first layer, and the error compounds through 31 more.

A worked case, and why the error compounds. After one layer the pre-activation really is close to Gaussian, because it is a weighted sum of many independent inputs and the central limit theorem applies. After the ReLU it is not: half the mass sits at exactly zero. The next layer sums many of those, which pushes it back toward Gaussian, but not all the way, and the residue is correlated across neurons because they share inputs. Repeat thirty-two times and the correlation, not the shape of any single marginal, is what dominates. Section 6 gives the measurement: exact marginals at every layer with the closure's own correlations still leaves you at 7.07e-7.

Concept

Control variate

A way to reduce the variance of an average without changing what it converges to. Suppose you want the mean of f, and you have a second quantity g whose true mean you already know exactly. Then for any coefficient b, the quantity f - b(g - E[g]) has the same mean as f, because the subtracted part averages to zero.

If g is correlated with f, choosing b well cancels part of the noise. The best possible choice removes a fraction equal to the squared correlation between them, so a control correlating at 0.8 with the target removes 64 percent of the variance.

The catch, and the subject of section 9, is that the control can only remove variance that is still there. On a structured point set the low-order part of the space has already been integrated almost exactly, so a control built from low-order functions of the input finds its target gone.

The pilot pruning rule

Run a small prefix of the rows first, look at the largest pre-activation each neuron achieved on them, and skip the neurons that never came close to firing. The rule is a margin on the layer's own scale rather than a probability:

C5_TAU = 0.010 # margin, as a fraction of the layer's own maximum C5_N1 = 472 # pilot rows, out of 98,304 C5_L0 = 4 # first layer the split runs on mzl = fnp.max(zp, axis=0).tolist() # best pilot value per column mx = max(mzl) thr = -C5_TAU * (mx if mx >= 0.0 else -mx) al = [j for j, v in enumerate(mzl) if v > thr] # the alive set

Everything outside al is never computed for the remaining 97,832 rows. Measured effect on billed cost: -7.86 percent.

Once columns are pruned, the remaining ones have to be selected out of the weight matrix. Two spellings give the identical result and the meter prices them very differently: a fancy gather bills 4.0 per element written, a delete bills 1.0.

# the expensive spelling: take() is the access tier, 4.0 per element z = _td(fnp.take(hf, keep_cols, axis=-1), w_kept) # the cheap spelling: delete the complement instead, 1.0 per element z = _mm(fnp.delete(hf, drops[0], -1), _rows(wp_use, 0, hot))

Same arithmetic, same output, a quarter of the billed cost on that operation. On one rig network this moved 2.63e9 billed operations to 0.75e9, which is -1.47 percent of total cost, at the price of about 46 extra round trips into the library.

Concept

FLOPs and the metered sandbox

A floating point operation, used here as the unit of account. Submissions run against a metered work-alike for the usual numerical library, which counts every arithmetic operation you issue and bills you for it according to a published price list.

The pricing is more detailed than a naive count. A matrix product of shape (k,m) by (m,o) bills exactly k·o·(2m-1). Elementwise operations bill 1.0 per element. Data movement is not free: reshape, copy and concatenate bill 1.0 per element, and a gather bills 4.0 per element written. Float64 bills at twice the rate of float32, and float16 gets no discount over float32.

The practical consequence is that the shape of your code, not just its mathematics, shows up in your score. Several published techniques are purely about spelling the same computation in a cheaper billed form, and one independent audit found that the obvious arbitrage between different contraction spellings does not exist: they all bill identically.

A worked case. One dense layer here is a 256 by 256 matrix against a batch of samples. For a single sample that is 256 × (2 × 256 - 1) = 130,816 billed operations, and thirty-two layers of it is about 4.19e6 per sample. The budget of 2.72e11 therefore buys roughly 65,000 samples if you do the obvious thing. My own build roughly halves that price once the cost-model defect described in section 7 is removed. Its 98,304 rows are not set by the price, though: they are set by the 50 MiB cap on the shipped asset and by the wall-clock guard.

The radial-angular split

Write a Gaussian input as its length times its direction, X = R·U, where U is uniform on the unit sphere and R is the length, independent of U. Because a bias-free ReLU network is positively homogeneous, scaling the input scales every activation by the same factor. So the expectation factorises exactly:

E[ f(X) ] = E[R] × E[ f(U) ]

The radial part E[R] is a known constant that you compute once, in closed form, for free. All the sampling effort goes into the directional part, on the sphere. You have removed one entire source of variance at no cost and introduced no error.

This turned out to be the common foundation under most of the strongest published entries. It is also the precondition for using spherical designs, because a spherical design is a rule for integrating over directions, and it only helps if the radius has already been handled exactly.

Spherical 5-designs from Kerdock codes

A spherical t-design is a finite set of directions with the property that averaging any polynomial of degree at most t over the set gives exactly the same answer as integrating it over the whole sphere. Not approximately. Exactly. If your integrand is well approximated by a low-degree polynomial, a design integrates it far better than random points of the same count.

The construction several participants converged on comes from coding theory. Kerdock codes yield a maximal set of mutually unbiased bases, which assembles into a 5-design of 66,048 directions in 256 dimensions. Participants reported this is only 0.389 percent above the Delsarte-Goethals-Seidel lower bound of 65,792 nodes, meaning it is very close to the smallest 5-design that can exist at this dimension.

Reported ablations at matched cost put independent Gaussian draws well behind the design. One author's sampling-geometry ablation at 74,752 rows gives independent draws a raw error of 7.4115e-7 against the design baseline. Another reports the depth-32 angular discrepancy at 2.4336603575430052e-7, evaluated twice at 120 decimal digits.

Also worth knowing: the same author reports that the budget forecloses ever doing better with a uniform design. Strength 6 would need 2,861,952 nodes, about 38 times more, and the whole budget spent linearly on directions buys roughly 141,185. The next rung up does not fit.

My own build used scrambled Sobol points in the input space rather than a spherical design on directions. Whether that was the wrong choice is genuinely unsettled in the public record: one participant measured Sobol worse than plain independent draws at equal row count, another shipped a Sobol stream and reported it beating shifted replicates, and a third closed lattice methods entirely. See the card on what the carrier is actually doing, which complicates all of this.

Antipodal pairing, and a genuine disagreement

If you evaluate a direction and also its exact negation, the ReLU identity ReLU(-y) = ReLU(y) - y lets you obtain the second from the first almost for free at the first layer, and the pair cancels all odd-degree error terms. It looks like a free factor of two.

The published record does not agree on what it is worth, and this is worth showing rather than smoothing over:

  • One participant measured that removing antipodes from their carrier made results 119 times worse in a matched raw-cost comparison, calling it the strongest single effect they found. Their own caveat is that removing the antipodes also changes what is being integrated, so this is not a clean isolation of the pairing.
  • Another measured antipodal pairing on a lattice making things worse: raw error 8.491e-7 rising to 1.091e-6 at essentially unchanged compute.
  • My own measurement puts the standalone gain at about 1.07 to 1.10 times, and I attribute the cap to the arc-cosine angle map. A third participant reaches a similar magnitude by a different route, reporting that antipodal pairing annihilates the odd Hermite degrees, which carry about 54 percent of the variance.

The likely resolution is that the effect is carrier-dependent: what pairing is worth depends entirely on what point set you are pairing within, so these three results may all be correct about different constructions. Nobody has run the crossed experiment that would settle it.

Batch whitening

Draw your batch of inputs, then measure the sample covariance the batch actually has. It will not be exactly the identity, because a finite sample never is. Apply a linear transform that forces it to be the identity, and use the corrected batch.

The idea is that you are removing the error you can see rather than the error you expect. Standard variance reduction removes anticipated variance using a model; whitening removes the realised discrepancy of this particular draw, which requires no model at all.

Reported effects, on the Monte Carlo branch: whitening alone gives 2.06× to 2.34×, and whitening combined with antipodal pairing gives 2.86×. The same author reports that forcing the point set to be spherical on top of whitening is harmful, at 0.74×, and explicitly retracted an earlier claim in that direction.

The stronger version of the claim comes from a different author, in topic 18151, in a four-arm experiment over 8 networks and 16 draws: lattice with sphere and whitening 3.5252e-07, independent draws with sphere and whitening 3.6242e-07, independent with sphere 7.0702e-07, plain independent 6.7406e-07. Reading across, the lattice buys 0.97× ± 0.07 once both arms are whitened, meaning nothing, while whitening buys 2.00× ± 0.32, meaning the entire effect.

The argument for why is a counting one. A low-discrepancy point set is a set whose low-order moments are nearly exact. Once whitening makes the second moment exactly right, the point set has nothing left to supply. Antipodal pairing removes the odd degrees, whitening removes degree two, and only degree four and above is left to buy, which the same author prices at needing about eight times the sample count.

If that holds, it partly explains my own null result on control variates and it means the quasi-Monte Carlo apparatus my build is organised around was the wrong axis to spend on. This is the single thing on this page I most regret not testing.

Mid-network control variates

A control variate is a quantity you can compute two ways: approximately but cheaply, and exactly. The difference between the two tells you which direction your cheap estimate is off, and you subtract a scaled version of that difference from your answer.

The insight here is where to place it. The analytic closure is accurate in the early layers and useless in the late ones. So run both the sampler and the cheap analytic model, compare them at an intermediate layer where the analytic model is still trustworthy, and use that discrepancy to correct the final answer. You are buying dependence information without paying for a sample.

Reported results: one team measured a 17.6 percent reduction in final-layer error using a layer-9 comparison, winning on 34 of 50 development networks with a paired bootstrap confidence interval. Another reported 2.39 times leak-free variance reduction using a leave-one-half-out split. A third deployed a layer-22 control with a learned centre.

The catch, which two of the three flag: fitting anything on the same batch you are correcting introduces contamination, and one author measured a correlation of 0.885 between the corrector's output and the batch noise it was supposed to be independent of.

Cancel the rule's error, not the sampler's variance

This is a design principle rather than a technique, and it explains a class of failures including one of my own.

On independent random draws, your error is variance, and a control variate that correlates with the integrand reduces it. On a structured point set such as a scrambled digital net or a spherical design, the point set has already removed most of that variance. What remains is the quadrature rule's own error, which is a different mathematical object living on a different set of basis functions.

So a control built to cancel Monte Carlo variance will find that its target has already been removed, and it will return nothing. That is exactly what I measured: a control built from the input coordinates removed 33 percent of the variance on independent draws and nothing resolvable on a scrambled net, at a variance ratio of 1.0052 against a resolution limit near 5 percent.

The corollary is constructive. If you use a structured point set, your control has to be designed against the structure of that specific rule's residual error, not against the integrand.

Hermite series instead of quadrature

An estimator that propagates a full covariance needs, at every layer, the expectation of ReLU(a)·ReLU(b) for a pair of correlated Gaussians. Computing that by numerical quadrature is accurate and expensive.

There is a classical alternative. Mehler's formula expands the bivariate Gaussian density in Hermite polynomials, which turns the pair integral into a series in powers of the correlation whose coefficients are known in closed form. Truncating the series at a handful of terms gives all the accuracy the surrounding method can use.

The reported cost difference is the striking part. Thirty-one layers of quadrature bills 8.32e10 FLOPs, which is 30.6 percent of the entire per-network budget. Thirty-one layers of the series at eight terms bills 3.31e07, which is 0.012 percent. Roughly two and a half thousand times cheaper for the same quantity.

This is a good example of a technique that is not novel mathematics at all, and is still worth a great deal, because the metric charges for arithmetic and the series does less of it.

Maximum-entropy readout

Suppose you know the first six moments of the final layer's pre-activation distribution exactly, and you need the expected ReLU. The classical move is an Edgeworth or Gram-Charlier expansion, which writes the unknown density as a Gaussian times a polynomial correction. These are known to misbehave: the implied density can go negative in the tails, which is precisely where a ReLU expectation is sensitive.

The maximum-entropy alternative asks a different question. Among all probability densities consistent with the moments you know, take the one with the highest entropy, meaning the one that assumes the least beyond your constraints. It is an exponential of a polynomial, so it is positive everywhere by construction.

Reported measurement: squared bias of 5.077e-09 at order six, stable to 0.7 percent across a fourfold change in how the moments were computed, improving to 8.148e-10 at order eight though converging for only 85 percent of cases. Both beat the series expansions on the same inputs.

Worth reading alongside section 6 of this page, which argues that the readout is only about 0.03 percent of the total error. This technique is excellent and its ceiling is low, and both things are true at once.

Sixty-six neurons carry the problem

The error is not spread evenly across the 256 output neurons. Each neuron has a standardised distance from the ReLU kink, the quantity called alpha in the closure recurrence. A neuron sitting far above the kink is effectively linear and easy. One far below is effectively dead and also easy. Only neurons near the kink are genuinely hard, because that is where the expectation depends on the fine shape of the distribution rather than on its first moment.

The reported census over four networks and 1024 neurons gives quantiles of the absolute standardised distance at 0.36 for the 5th percentile, 1.93 at the 25th, 3.49 at the median, 4.32 at the 75th and 5.04 at the 95th. Most neurons are comfortably far from the kink. The error concentrates in the small population that is not.

The practical reading is that a method which is only accurate on the hard minority, and cheap elsewhere, is spending its budget correctly. Several of the pruning and certification techniques in this competition, including two in my own build, are exactly that idea applied to cost rather than to accuracy.

Every spelling of a contraction bills the same

An obvious thing to try, once you know the score is accuracy times billed cost, is to look for a cheaper way to spell the same matrix multiplication. Numerical libraries expose many entry points that all compute contractions.

Several participants tested this systematically, and the answer is that there is nothing there. matmul, dot, einsum with and without optimisation, tensordot, inner and multi_dot all bill identically for the same mathematical work, verified across many shapes. Float16 bills the same as float32, and float64 bills double. One participant conducted a direct audit of the evaluation-version cost model and reported zero arbitrage found across all routes.

The honest exception in my own case was a defect rather than a discount: padding arrays past a rank threshold routed contractions to a shape-only estimate that billed about half the honest charge. I reported it and elected to have it patched. It is documented here so nobody mistakes it for a technique.

The genuine savings in this competition come from doing less arithmetic, not from spelling the same arithmetic differently.

The symmetric Gram discount

When you contract a matrix with itself to form a Gram matrix, the result is symmetric, so only about half of the output entries are independent. The meter recognises this and prices it accordingly.

Reported measurement at width 256 with 36,356 rows: 2,391,901,056 billed against 4,765,188,096 for the equivalent general contraction. That is 0.502×, and the results are bit-identical with a maximum difference of exactly zero.

This is a real discount rather than a defect, because the symmetric product genuinely does half the independent work. The precondition matters, and without it this card contradicts the one about every spelling billing the same. The discount fires for a repeated-operand contraction, meaning the two operands are literally the same array rather than merely equal in value; written any other way the identical product bills in full. So the lesson is narrow: if the expensive step can be restructured into a genuine contraction of an array with itself, the meter will halve it. One participant bundled this with a half-sample coefficient fit and reported cutting billed cost meaningfully at exactly zero statistical cost.

Wall time is a per-call tax

The score charges you for residual wall-clock time at a hundred billion FLOPs per second, so it looks like you should minimise time spent outside the metered library. The natural conclusion is to batch aggressively: fewer, larger operations.

The measurement says the first half of that is right and the reason is different from what you would guess. The residual charge is dominated by how many times you call in, not by how much work each call does. Reported figures: about 3.3 microseconds per instrumented call for tiny operands, 13.2 microseconds at 54 MB with a destination array supplied, and 6250 microseconds when a fresh 54 MB buffer has to be allocated. One author measured wall time at essentially flat across a sixfold change in the mathematical work per call: 1.46, 1.50 and 1.74 seconds.

The practical rules that follow are specific. Reduce call count rather than operation count when those conflict. Supply destination arrays so buffers are reused rather than allocated. Be aware that a self-check helper whose cost grows with process history becomes unaffordable by the end of a long grading run.

Strassen has a hard ceiling

Strassen's algorithm multiplies two matrices using seven products instead of eight, at the cost of extra additions, and applying it recursively compounds the saving. Since the meter charges for arithmetic, this is a direct score improvement.

It converges, and quickly. The reported cumulative saving across a 32-layer chain, by recursion depth, is 1.000, 1.138, 1.288, 1.444, 1.588, 1.686, then 1.686 again. Depth five and depth six give the same number. Deeper recursion adds function calls, and by the per-call tax above, those calls cost wall time that is charged back.

My own build stops at two levels for the same reason: a third level measured +1.28 percent billed cost. The float64 promotion trap

Float64 bills at twice the rate of float32, and float16 gets no discount over float32, so float32 is the correct working precision throughout. That is easy to say and hard to enforce, because a single helper that silently promotes will poison everything downstream of it.

The specific trap reported: the statistics helpers in the metered library return float64 for any input, including float32 input. A participant reported that switching to a float32 pass was worth only 0.7 percent of their bill until they found and cast back three width-sized vectors per layer, at which point the saving appeared.

A related and more dangerous trap in the same family: one participant reported that a validation path made a float32 assumption fail in a way that moved their proxy score from 3.6e-7 to 6.6e-2, a catastrophic five-order-of-magnitude regression, with a two-operation workaround. If you are optimising precision, check the actual dtype of every intermediate rather than trusting the declared one.

The budget cliff, enforced late

Exceeding the compute budget is not a graded penalty. When effective compute exceeds the budget, the grader zeroes your predictions for that network, which turns a small overspend into a total loss on that instance.

The complication is historical. Participants documented that this check was not actually wired on the evaluator until around 9 August 2026, and that submissions made under the unenforced rule were then re-graded retroactively. One documented case went from reading in the region of 4e-8 per network to an aggregate of 0.682, with 49 of the public networks failed.

Two things follow. First, leave real headroom rather than tuning to the edge of the budget, because the failure mode is discontinuous. My own honest twin sits about 0.3 seconds of wall-clock from the boundary on its worst network, which is closer than I would choose again. Second, treat any leaderboard snapshot from before the patch as having been produced under different rules.

Close a family without building it

This is the highest-value technique on this page and it is a research protocol rather than an algorithm.

Take your estimator. Replace one internal quantity with its ground-truth value, taken from the answer key. Leave everything else running unchanged. Measure the error that remains. That number is an upper bound on every possible idea whose effect passes through that quantity, because no method can do better on that quantity than being exactly right about it.

If the bound sits above your target, the entire family is closed. Not "probably not worth it": closed, by measurement, including ideas nobody has written down yet. The author reports each such measurement cost about fifteen minutes on one GPU and that they closed 54 families of estimators this way without building any of them.

I used the same instrument independently and it produced the most important result in my own campaign, the finding in section 6 that propagation rather than readout carries nearly all the error. It is the single thing I would tell a newcomer to build first.

The answer key is itself noisy

The targets you are scored against were produced by Monte Carlo at a billion samples. That is a lot of samples, but it is not infinity, so the answer key carries its own error.

The reported floor is 4.949e-11 on the mini split and 5.219e-11 on the full split. Any measured difference below that level is measuring the target's noise rather than your estimator's accuracy.

This matters more than it sounds. It sets a hard limit on what can ever be demonstrated, it means two independent replications of the ground truth will disagree with each other, and it means an ablation that looks like a small win may be reading noise in the labels. A separate participant, in topic 18149, reported that two truth replicates they had baked locally disagreed with each other by more than the effect they were trying to measure. That is about their own reference rather than the competition's answer key, and it makes the same point: check the label noise before trusting a small win.

Rotation noise swamps small effects

Because the input distribution is spherically symmetric, you can rotate the whole problem and it is the same problem. That gives you a free source of replicates, and it also reveals how noisy a single measurement is.

The reported finding: a single network's error varied by a factor of roughly 2.9 to 5.4 across ten global orientations, with split-half rank correlation low enough that per-network rankings are not stable under rotation. The right answer for the wrong reason

Most of the field converged on the same 66,048-direction carrier and explained it the same way: it is a spherical 5-design, so it integrates every polynomial up to degree five exactly, and the integrand is well approximated by a low-degree polynomial.

One participant noticed that the explanation was testable. Real mutually unbiased bases in 256 dimensions max out at 129, not 128, and the commonly used construction was missing one. Adding it takes the rule from 65,536 to 66,048 signed paths, a factor of exactly 1.0078125, and that completion is precisely what makes the design tight. If degree-five exactness were the mechanism, completing the design should buy a great deal.

It buys almost nothing. The reported completion gain is 1.0091 on a held-out lockbox and 1.0128 on validation, and after normalising for the 0.78 percent extra paths, only 1.0050× on validation and 1.0012× on the lockbox. The author's proposed replacement explanation is that mutual unbiasedness, not polynomial exactness, is what minimises the quadrature risk for this particular kernel.

I have kept this card for what it shows about method rather than about carriers. An entire field can hold a correct empirical result attached to an incorrect causal story, and the thing that separates the two is somebody constructing the experiment where the story makes a prediction.

The exactly correct mask loses to a sloppy one

Pruning is the standard cheap win. Run a small pilot batch, note which neurons never fire, and skip them for the remaining samples. My own build does a version of this.

Two results here are worth sitting with. The first is that pruning is a cliff and not a slope. As the pilot firing threshold rises from 1 to 64 out of 1,024 pilot paths, retained width falls smoothly from about 214.7 to 184.3 of 256, but the error does not follow smoothly. It stays flat at roughly 2.24e-7 to 2.48e-7 through threshold 8, then jumps to 4.67e-7 at 16, 1.89e-6 at 32 and 1.30e-5 at 64. Threshold 16 is only 7.2 percent narrower and carries 2.08 times the error. Rarely firing units define activation cones that later layers depend on.

The second is genuinely counterintuitive. At matched compute, a pilot pruner that misclassifies some live neurons as dead beats an oracle that knows exactly which neurons are truly dead: no pruning 1.7485e-07 as the baseline, a 512-point pilot 1.3293e-07 at 1.315 times better and winning 7 of 8 networks, and the exact oracle mask 1.4513e-07 at 1.205 times.

The explanation offered is that a unit firing once in five hundred inputs contributes almost nothing to the mean and a full unit's worth of variance, so dropping it is a favourable trade that the exact mask refuses to make. Correctness and usefulness are different objectives here, and the metric rewards the second.

An exact cubature rule, seventy times worse

If exact degree-five integration on the sphere helps, an exact degree-five cubature rule for the Gaussian ought to help too. A textbook construction at dimension 256 uses 66,307 nodes, almost exactly the same count as the carrier the field was already using, which makes it a clean head to head.

The author built it and verified it: exact to a worst relative error of 1.835e-15 over every monomial of degree five or below, and correctly failing at degree six. Then measured it. Over 128 pairs at matched node count, whitening plus antipodal pairing gives 3.6955e-07 and the exact degree-five rule gives 2.6048e-05. That is 70.5 times worse, at t = 15.92.

The reason is structural rather than incidental. At any dimension above seven, degree-five exactness algebraically forces at least one negative weight. The rule becomes an extrapolation between a small simplex block and a large midpoint block, and the effective sample size collapses to 941 of 66,306 nodes, a factor of 0.0142. The author attributes about 47 of the 70 times purely to forced weight negativity.

A companion result from the same author, in the same spirit: an exactly correct multilevel telescoping identity over depth, with no approximation at any level, still loses to plain Monte Carlo by 12.3 times, because Monte Carlo serves every level from a single forward pass while the telescope must split its samples across levels.

The lesson generalises past this competition. Exactness, whether of a quadrature rule or of an identity, constrains the bias and says nothing at all about the variance, and under a squared-error metric the variance is usually what you are paying for.
